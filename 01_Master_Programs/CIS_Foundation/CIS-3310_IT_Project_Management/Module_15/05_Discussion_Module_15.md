## Scenario 1: "We Do Agile But Nothing Changed"

Greenfield Insurance hired an Agile coach six months ago and officially transitioned
all IT development teams to Scrum. They renamed their project phases to sprints, their
weekly status meetings to Daily Scrums, and their project managers to Scrum Masters.
However, the Scrum Masters still assign tasks to individual developers. The Product
Owner role was assigned to the IT Director, who attends Sprint Reviews but has not
updated the product backlog in two months. Developers still receive their requirements
as detailed specifications written by a separate business analyst team. Velocity data
is included in executive dashboards as a productivity metric.

Team morale is unchanged and delivery timelines have not improved.

### Initial Post Prompt

Diagnose what went wrong with Greenfield's Agile transition. Identify at least three
specific practices that are inconsistent with Agile values and Scrum principles, and
explain what the correct practice should look like. Why does simply renaming activities
fail to produce Agile benefits? Your response should be 200–225 words.

### Model Response (200–225 words)

Greenfield performed what practitioners call a "cargo cult Agile" transformation —
they adopted the vocabulary and ceremony names of Scrum without adopting the underlying
values and principles that make Scrum work. Three specific violations stand out.

First, Scrum Masters assigning tasks directly contradicts the self-organizing team
principle. In authentic Scrum, the development team pulls work from the sprint backlog
and self-assigns based on skill and capacity. The Scrum Master's job is to remove
impediments, not direct labor. Correct practice: facilitate sprint planning so the
team commits to the sprint goal and self-organizes around it.

Second, the Product Owner role is hollow. An IT Director who attends reviews but does
not maintain the backlog cannot fulfill the PO's core accountability: continuously
ordering the backlog to maximize value. Correct practice: the PO should review and
re-prioritize the backlog before every sprint planning session, incorporating feedback
from the sprint review.

Third, using velocity as an executive productivity metric creates perverse incentives.
Teams will inflate story point estimates to show rising velocity rather than delivering
more value. Correct practice: velocity is shared with the team for planning purposes
only; executives should track business outcomes, not story points.

Renaming activities without changing authority, accountability, and collaboration
structures simply adds ceremony overhead to the same dysfunctional processes.

three are equally damaging? Which single failure do you think causes the most harm

## Scenario 2: Waterfall vs. Agile — Choosing the Right Fit

ClearView Analytics is evaluating two upcoming projects and needs to decide which
methodology to apply to each.

Project A: Redesign of an internal employee expense reporting tool. Stakeholders have
provided detailed requirements, the UI design is approved, and the integration with the
existing ERP system is well-documented. The project has a hard deadline tied to a
new fiscal year accounting cycle.

Project B: Development of a new customer-facing data visualization product. The exact
feature set is unknown — ClearView plans to run user research sessions during
development and incorporate findings into the product. The target market has never
used a similar product, so stakeholder expectations are uncertain.

### Initial Post Prompt

Recommend a project management approach for each project — waterfall, Agile (Scrum or
Kanban), or hybrid — and justify your recommendation. Apply specific criteria from the
module to support your reasoning. Do not simply assert that "Agile is always better"
or "waterfall is outdated" — engage with the actual project characteristics. Your
response should be 200–225 words.

### Model Response (200–225 words)

Project A is a strong candidate for waterfall or a light hybrid approach. The defining
characteristics favor a sequential model: requirements are detailed and approved, the
integration is well-documented, the UI design is finalized, and there is a hard
non-negotiable deadline. Waterfall's strengths — predictability, clear milestones, and
front-loaded documentation — align directly with these conditions. A hybrid variant
could use Agile sprints for the development phase while keeping formal stage gates for
requirements sign-off and UAT, satisfying governance without sacrificing timeline
control.

Project B is a strong candidate for Scrum. The defining characteristics favor an
iterative model: the feature set is unknown, requirements will emerge from user research
conducted during development, and stakeholder expectations are uncertain. Scrum's
sprint-by-sprint delivery model allows ClearView to build, test with real users, learn,
and adjust the product backlog continuously. Sprint Reviews become the primary
mechanism for incorporating user research findings. Delivering early working increments
also reduces the risk of building an entire product that misses the market.

The module's lesson here is that approach selection is a risk management decision.
Waterfall reduces execution risk when requirements are stable. Agile reduces discovery
risk when requirements are uncertain. Forcing Agile onto Project A or waterfall onto
Project B would introduce the risks the wrong methodology is least equipped to handle.

## Scenario 3: The Sprint That Would Not End

The Scrum team at Meridian Healthcare has been in Sprint 4 for three weeks. The sprint
was originally planned as a two-week sprint. The team extended it because two user
stories were not complete at the end of Week 2. The Product Owner agreed to the
extension to avoid carrying unfinished work into Sprint 5.

Now, entering Week 3, one of the extended stories is still incomplete because the
developer assigned to it discovered the story is much larger than estimated. The Scrum
Master is considering extending the sprint a fourth week.

Other team members are frustrated. They have completed their own stories and are
sitting idle, unsure whether to start Sprint 5 backlog items or wait.

### Initial Post Prompt

What went wrong in this scenario and what should the Scrum Master do now? Address the
Scrum principle that sprints are fixed-length time boxes and explain why violating this
principle creates the cascading problems visible in this scenario. What should the team
do with the incomplete story? What should have happened differently at sprint planning?
Your response should be 200–225 words.

### Model Response (200–225 words)

This scenario illustrates what happens when the sprint time box — one of Scrum's most
important structural constraints — is treated as flexible. The sprint's fixed length
is not arbitrary. It creates a regular cadence of planning, execution, review, and
retrospection that the entire organization can depend on. When sprints stretch, the
Sprint Review is delayed, stakeholder feedback is withheld, retrospective improvements
are deferred, and idle team members accumulate waste time. The downstream sprints are
compressed or the release date slips.

The Scrum Master should not extend to Week 4. The correct action now is to end the
sprint, hold the Sprint Review with the completed stories, cancel the incomplete story,
and carry it — re-estimated — into the Sprint 5 backlog at the correct priority.

The incomplete story should be split. If it proved larger than estimated, it should
be decomposed into smaller stories that individually fit within a sprint, which is
exactly what the INVEST "Small" criterion requires.

At sprint planning, the team should have applied more scrutiny to the 13-point stories
in the backlog. Stories above 8 points are often too large for confident estimation.
The team should have spiked or decomposed large stories before committing them to a
sprint.

The Product Owner's instinct to avoid carry-over work is understandable but
counterproductive when it causes a worse structural failure.

## Professor Nash's Note

Scenario 1 is the most common Agile failure pattern I have seen in real organizations.
The instinct to map old roles and activities onto new names is understandable — change
is uncomfortable and renaming feels like progress. But Agile requires a genuine shift
in authority and accountability, not just vocabulary.

The most important question to ask when evaluating any Agile implementation is: who
has the power to say no to scope changes, and do they use it? If the Product Owner
cannot say no to the IT Director's last-minute feature requests, the backlog is not
real, and sprint planning is theater.

Scenario 3 is the second most common failure. Fixed time boxes feel inefficient when
work is not done. The instinct is to extend. Resist it. The value of the time box is
in the discipline, not the duration. A team that routinely carries work across sprint
boundaries is signaling an estimation problem, a scope problem, or a capacity problem
— and all three are diagnosable and fixable. Extending the sprint hides the signal.
