## Segment 2 — The Procurement Process (2:30–6:30)

[SHOW SLIDE: "Four Procurement Processes"]

PMI's framework defines four procurement management processes. Plan Procurement Management happens in Planning — you decide what to buy, how to buy it, and what type of contract to use. Conduct Procurements happens in Executing — you solicit bids, evaluate proposals, and award the contract. Control Procurements happens in Monitoring and Controlling — you manage the contract relationship, verify deliverables, and process changes. Close Procurements happens in Closing — you formally complete the contract and archive the documentation.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

[SHOW SLIDE: "Make-or-Buy Analysis"]

Before any procurement begins, the project team performs a make-or-buy analysis. This is a systematic evaluation of whether it is more cost-effective and strategically appropriate to perform the work internally or to acquire it from an external source.

The make-or-buy decision is not purely a cost calculation. It also considers availability of internal expertise, strategic competency (you may not want a competitor to learn your internal processes), vendor specialization, speed, and capacity constraints.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

For example: a telecom company building a network monitoring dashboard might make the core application internally because it integrates proprietary data — but buy the underlying cloud infrastructure because they lack expertise and the vendor can deliver it faster.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Plan Procurement Management — Key Outputs"]

The primary output of Plan Procurement Management is the Procurement Management Plan. This subsidiary plan documents the procurement strategy, contract types to be used, procurement authority, how proposals will be evaluated, and risk allocation decisions. It also produces the Statement of Work, which we will cover next.

## Segment 3 — The Statement of Work and Solicitation Documents (6:30–10:30)

[SHOW SLIDE: "Statement of Work (SOW)"]

The Statement of Work — or SOW — is the document that tells a prospective vendor exactly what they are being asked to deliver. A well-written SOW is specific, measurable, and unambiguous. A vague SOW is an invitation for disputes.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

The SOW typically includes: a description of the work to be performed, the deliverables required, any technical specifications or standards that must be met, the schedule expectations, the acceptance criteria that determine when the work is considered complete, and any conditions of performance such as location, security requirements, or reporting obligations.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Solicitation Document Types"]

Once the SOW is drafted, the organization issues a solicitation document to invite vendors to respond. The three solicitation types you need to know for the Project+ exam are:

Request for Proposal — RFP. Used when you want vendors to propose how they will solve the problem. The buyer has defined what they need but is open to different approaches. RFPs typically evaluate both price and technical approach.

Request for Quotation — RFQ. Used when the requirement is well-defined and standardized. You know exactly what you want; you are asking for a price. Commodity purchases and equipment procurement typically use RFQs.

Invitation for Bid — IFB. Similar to an RFQ but typically used in formal government procurement. The requirement is fully specified and award goes to the lowest compliant bidder. Evaluation is price only.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

[SHOW SLIDE: "Source Selection Criteria"]

After vendors respond to the solicitation, the buyer evaluates proposals using source selection criteria. These are the weighted factors used to compare vendors. For complex IT procurements, source selection criteria commonly include technical approach and methodology, vendor experience and past performance, proposed staffing and key personnel qualifications, price, and transition and implementation approach.

Using a weighted scoring matrix ensures the evaluation is systematic and defensible. Each criterion is given a weight, and each proposal is scored against every criterion. The vendor with the highest weighted total score is recommended for award.

## Segment 4 — Contract Types (10:30–16:00)

[SHOW SLIDE: "Three Contract Type Families"]

The most heavily tested topic in procurement management is contract types. Every contract type represents a different risk allocation between the buyer and the seller. There are three families: Fixed-Price, Cost-Reimbursable, and Time and Materials. Let me walk through each one in detail.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Fixed-Price Contracts"]

In a Fixed-Price contract, the buyer agrees to pay a set price regardless of the seller's actual costs. The seller assumes the cost risk — if their costs come in higher than expected, the seller absorbs the difference. If they deliver under cost, the seller keeps the savings.

Fixed-Price contracts are appropriate when the scope of work is well-defined and stable. If the requirements are clear, the seller can price the work accurately and both parties benefit from certainty.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

There are three variants. Firm Fixed-Price — FFP — is the simplest: one price, no adjustments. Fixed-Price Incentive Fee — FPIF — adds a performance bonus if the seller meets or beats targets. Fixed-Price with Economic Price Adjustment — FPEPA — allows for price adjustments tied to external indices like inflation; used on long-duration contracts where cost escalation is a legitimate risk.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Cost-Reimbursable Contracts"]

In a Cost-Reimbursable contract, the buyer reimburses the seller's actual allowable costs plus a fee. The buyer assumes the cost risk — if the project runs long or expensive, the buyer pays. Cost-Reimbursable contracts are appropriate when the scope is uncertain or difficult to define in advance, such as research and development work or early-phase discovery projects.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

The three variants: Cost Plus Fixed Fee — CPFF — pays actual costs plus a fixed fee that does not change based on performance. Cost Plus Incentive Fee — CPIF — adds a bonus for meeting targets; the buyer and seller share cost savings based on a pre-agreed formula. Cost Plus Award Fee — CPAF — gives the buyer discretion to award a fee based on subjective performance criteria.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Time and Materials Contracts"]

Time and Materials — T&M — is a hybrid. The buyer pays a fixed rate per unit of time (labor) plus actual material costs. T&M is used when the scope is unclear but you need work to begin immediately. Consulting engagements, staff augmentation, and early-stage development work commonly use T&M contracts.

The risk in T&M is open-ended cost exposure — there is no ceiling unless the contract specifies one. Buyers often add a Not-to-Exceed clause to manage this risk.

[PAUSE — 3 seconds]

[SHOW SLIDE: "Contract Risk Summary"]

Here is how to remember risk allocation on the exam:

Fixed-Price — seller bears cost risk. Buyer has cost certainty.
Cost-Reimbursable — buyer bears cost risk. Seller has cost certainty.
Time and Materials — shared risk; buyer has open cost exposure unless capped.

The exam will describe a scenario and ask which contract type is most appropriate, or which party bears more risk. Anchor your answer to the scope clarity: clear scope points to Fixed-Price; unclear scope points to Cost-Reimbursable or T&M.

## Segment 5 — Conduct and Control Procurements (16:00–19:30)

[SHOW SLIDE: "Conduct Procurements — Executing Process"]

Conduct Procurements is the Executing process where the project team issues solicitation documents, receives and evaluates proposals, selects a vendor, and awards the contract. The key output is the signed agreement — the contract itself.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

Bidder conferences are a common tool in this process. A bidder conference brings all prospective vendors together to hear the requirements explained and ask questions simultaneously. This ensures all vendors receive the same information and the process remains fair and auditable.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Control Procurements — Monitoring and Controlling Process"]

Once the contract is signed, the project manager must manage the ongoing relationship. Control Procurements includes verifying that deliverables meet the contract requirements, approving payments when milestones are met, processing contract changes through the integrated change control process, and resolving disputes.

A critical point: contract changes must go through the procurement change control system, not just the internal project change control system. Unilateral changes — where one party modifies scope or requirements without the other party's agreement — are a common source of contract disputes.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Procurement Performance Reviews"]

The buyer monitors vendor performance through procurement performance reviews. These structured evaluations assess whether the vendor is on schedule, meeting quality standards, and within budget. Poor performance findings may trigger cure notices — formal requests for the vendor to correct deficiencies within a specified time.

## Segment 6 — Close Procurements and Exam Tips (19:30–22:00)

[SHOW SLIDE: "Close Procurements"]

Close Procurements is the formal completion of a contract. Key activities include verifying that all deliverables have been accepted, confirming all payments have been made, archiving contract records as organizational process assets, and releasing resources assigned to the procurement.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

Procurement closure and project closure are related but distinct. A project can have multiple procurements, and each must be formally closed. Procurement closure precedes final project closure.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Key Exam Distinctions"]

Four distinctions the Project+ exam frequently tests:

SOW versus Contract: the SOW describes what is to be delivered; the contract is the legally binding agreement that includes the SOW plus terms and conditions, pricing, dispute resolution mechanisms, and more.

RFP versus RFQ versus IFB: RFP invites solution proposals (approach + price); RFQ asks for price only on a defined item; IFB awards to the lowest compliant bidder in formal procurement.

Fixed-Price versus Cost-Reimbursable risk: Fixed-Price shifts risk to seller; Cost-Reimbursable retains risk with buyer.

Termination: contracts can be terminated for convenience (buyer decides to end the contract even without cause) or for cause (the seller has breached contract requirements). Both require formal notice and documentation.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Module 14 Key Takeaways"]

Procurement management begins with a make-or-buy decision and flows through Plan, Conduct, Control, and Close. The SOW defines what is being purchased. Solicitation document types — RFP, RFQ, IFB — match different purchase situations. Contract types allocate risk: Fixed-Price to the seller, Cost-Reimbursable to the buyer, T&M as a hybrid with open cost exposure. Control Procurements manages the ongoing relationship; Close Procurements finalizes it.
