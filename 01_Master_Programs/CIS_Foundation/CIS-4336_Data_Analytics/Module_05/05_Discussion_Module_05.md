## Scenario A — Reporting Salary Data to Leadership

A regional hospital has 320 employees. The HR analytics team computes annual salary statistics:

- Mean: $87,400
- Median: $58,200
- Standard deviation: $42,800
- IQR: $31,000

The CFO wants to include "average employee compensation" in the annual report. The CEO prefers to report a number that represents what a "typical" employee earns. A board member suggests just reporting the range.

In 175–225 words, address all three of the following:

1. Explain which statistic — mean, median, or range — best serves each executive's stated goal, and why. Use the relationship between the mean and median to explain what the gap tells you about the salary distribution.
2. The CFO argues that the mean is the "more correct" number because it accounts for all employees. Construct a counter-argument using the concepts of skewness and the sensitivity of mean to outliers.
3. If you were writing the executive summary, what two statistics would you report side by side, and what one-sentence explanation would you provide to a non-technical reader to help them interpret both numbers?

## Scenario B — Comparing Two Manufacturing Lines

A manufacturing company operates two production lines making the same component. Quality control measures the diameter (in millimeters) of sampled parts from each line over 30 days:

- Line A: Mean = 50.0 mm, Standard deviation = 0.3 mm
- Line B: Mean = 50.0 mm, Standard deviation = 2.1 mm

The target diameter is 50.0 mm. Both lines are meeting the target on average.

In 175–225 words, address all three of the following:

1. Both lines have identical means. Why does standard deviation matter here, and which line is performing better from a quality perspective? Use specific numeric reasoning.
2. A quality standard requires that 99.7% of parts fall within 3 standard deviations of the mean. Compute the acceptable range for each line and explain the practical manufacturing implication of the difference.
3. A manager proposes shutting down Line B because its standard deviation is "out of control." A statistician on the team says the mean is still on target and advises against shutdown. Who has the stronger argument, and what additional analysis would help resolve the disagreement?

## Scenario C — Interpreting a Skewed Distribution

A city's transportation department analyzes daily ridership counts on 365 bus routes. The distribution of daily ridership has the following characteristics:

- Mean: 1,240 riders per day
- Median: 680 riders per day
- Skewness value: 2.8
- A small number of high-demand express routes carry 8,000–12,000 riders per day

In 175–225 words, address all three of the following:

1. The skewness value of 2.8 indicates strong positive (right) skew. Explain in plain language what this means for the distribution of bus ridership across routes. Why is the mean so much higher than the median?
2. The department wants to plan bus fleet allocation by identifying "typical" route ridership. Should they use mean or median as their planning figure? Justify your answer using the specific statistics provided.
3. A data journalist writes: "The average bus route in this city carries 1,240 passengers per day." Is this statement misleading? How would you rewrite it to be both accurate and accessible to a general audience? What visualization would you recommend the journalist include to make the distribution transparent?
