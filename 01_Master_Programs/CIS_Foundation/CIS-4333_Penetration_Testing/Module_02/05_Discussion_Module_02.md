## Scenario A — The Liability Gap

A penetration tester completes an authorized external network test for a regional credit union. Three days after testing concludes, the credit union's IT team discovers that a production database server — which was in scope and was successfully exploited during the test — is now running corrupted and their most recent backup is 11 days old. The client claims the tester's exploitation caused the corruption. The tester has no memory of any action that would have corrupted the database, and the testing logs show only a read-only SQL injection proof of concept.

Discuss: What documentation should the tester have maintained during the engagement that would help resolve this dispute? How does the liability clause in the MSA interact with the RoE and the minimal footprint principle in this situation? What professional practice lessons does this scenario illustrate regardless of who is ultimately responsible for the corruption?

## Scenario B — The Whistleblower Dilemma

A penetration tester is conducting an authorized internal assessment for a publicly traded manufacturing company. While accessing a file server within the authorized scope, they discover a directory containing what appear to be financial documents showing the company has been systematically falsifying quarterly revenue reports. Nothing in the RoE addresses this scenario. The tester's firm has a long-standing business relationship with this client worth significant annual revenue.

Discuss: What are the tester's ethical and potential legal obligations in this situation? How do professional confidentiality obligations from the NDA interact with potential legal obligations around financial fraud? Does the financial value of the client relationship change the ethical calculation, and why or why not?

## Scenario C — The Cloud Permission Gap

A small business client engages your firm for a full penetration test of their environment. Their website, API, and customer database are all hosted on a popular cloud platform. The client signs a complete RoE and authorization letter. When you ask whether they have reviewed the cloud provider's penetration testing policy, the client says "we pay for the service, we own the data, we can authorize whatever we want."

Discuss: Is the client's reasoning legally correct? What specific risks does the testing firm face if they proceed without reviewing the cloud provider's policy? How would you explain the third-party authorization requirement to the client in a way that is professional and does not damage the relationship? What steps must occur before testing the cloud-hosted assets can begin?
