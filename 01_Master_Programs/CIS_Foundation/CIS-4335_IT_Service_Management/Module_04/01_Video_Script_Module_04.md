## [01:30 – 03:30] What Are the Guiding Principles?

The Guiding Principles are recommendations that guide an organization in all circumstances, regardless of changes in its goals, strategies, type of work, or management structure. ITIL 4 calls them "universal" for two reasons.

First, they apply at every level of the organization — from the board room to the front-line help desk analyst. They are not instructions only for IT leadership; they are recommendations for everyone involved in service management.

Second, they apply in every situation. They do not turn on and off depending on which practice you are using or which phase of a project you are in. Every time a decision needs to be made — about a process design, a tool selection, a communication strategy, or a service improvement — at least one Guiding Principle is relevant.

The Guiding Principles are not rules. You cannot violate them and be penalized. They are best-practice recommendations that, when followed, produce better outcomes. The Foundation exam tests whether you can recognize which principle is being applied or ignored in a given situation.

## [03:30 – 06:00] Principle 1: Focus on Value

The first Guiding Principle is Focus on Value. Everything the organization does should directly or indirectly create value for the organization, its customers, and other stakeholders.

The principle seems obvious — of course we should focus on value. But in practice, organizations frequently execute activities that consume resources without contributing to value. Meetings that exist because they have always existed. Reports that are generated but never read. Approval steps in workflows that add time without adding oversight. Compliance theater that creates documentation without creating security.

Focus on Value is the discipline of regularly asking: how does this activity connect to an outcome that stakeholders care about? If the answer is unclear, that is a signal to investigate.

For the exam: when a scenario describes an organization eliminating unnecessary activities, restructuring service delivery to better meet user needs, or reorienting a project around business outcomes rather than technical deliverables, Focus on Value is likely the applicable principle.

---

## [06:00 – 08:30] Principle 2: Start Where You Are

The second Guiding Principle is Start Where You Are. Do not start from scratch and build something new without first considering what is already available to be leveraged.

This principle addresses a common organizational failure mode: the assumption that existing capabilities are broken and must be replaced. When a new leader arrives, or a new framework is adopted, or a new project begins, there is often pressure to "start fresh." But starting fresh is expensive, disruptive, and risky. Existing processes, tools, relationships, and knowledge represent investments that should not be discarded without careful analysis.

Start Where You Are is about conducting an honest assessment of the current state before designing a new state. Measure and observe. Understand what currently works. Identify what specifically needs to change and why, rather than assuming everything must be replaced.

For the exam: when a scenario describes an organization assessing its current capabilities before implementing a new practice, or explicitly deciding not to replace a functional tool during a framework implementation, Start Where You Are is likely the applicable principle.

## [08:30 – 11:00] Principles 3 and 4: Progress Iteratively and Collaborate

Principle 3 is Progress Iteratively with Feedback. Do not attempt to do everything at once. Organize work into smaller, manageable sections that can be executed and completed in a timely manner.

This principle draws directly from Agile and Lean thinking. Rather than planning a massive 18-month implementation and hoping it succeeds, the principle recommends delivering in increments, gathering feedback after each increment, and using that feedback to adjust the next increment. This approach reduces risk, surfaces problems earlier, and maintains stakeholder engagement.

Feedback is a critical word in this principle. Iteration without feedback is just doing the same thing repeatedly. Iteration with feedback is learning and adapting.

Principle 4 is Collaborate and Promote Visibility. Inclusion is generally better than exclusion, and decisions made with appropriate information and involvement produce better results than those made in isolation.

This principle addresses silos — organizational boundaries that prevent information from flowing to where it is needed. In IT service management, silos are a primary cause of service failures. The development team deploys a change without telling operations. The operations team implements a workaround without telling the service desk. The service desk resolves incidents without sharing knowledge with problem management. The principle of Collaborate and Promote Visibility is the counter to all of these failure modes.

For the exam: when a scenario describes an organization piloting a new practice in one area before rolling it out broadly, that is Progress Iteratively with Feedback. When a scenario describes breaking down silos, involving stakeholders in decision-making, or making work and progress visible, that is Collaborate and Promote Visibility.

## [11:00 – 13:30] Principle 5: Think and Work Holistically

The fifth Guiding Principle is Think and Work Holistically. No service, practice, process, department, or supplier stands alone. All the elements of a service management environment work together in an integrated system, and each element affects and is affected by the others.

This principle is the antidote to tunnel vision. When a team redesigns the incident resolution process without considering how that change affects the problem management practice, the service desk scripts, or the knowledge management database, they have violated the Think and Work Holistically principle. The change may improve incident resolution metrics while degrading something else.

Think and Work Holistically requires keeping the whole system in view even when working on a specific component. It connects directly to the four dimensions framework — considering all four dimensions is an expression of holistic thinking.

For the exam: when a scenario describes an organization failing to consider the downstream effects of a change, or describes a system failure caused by optimization of one component at the expense of another, Think and Work Holistically is likely the applicable principle.

## [13:30 – 16:30] Principles 6 and 7: Keep It Simple and Optimize and Automate

Principle 6 is Keep It Simple and Practical. If a process, service, action, or metric fails to provide value or produce a useful outcome, eliminate it. Use the minimum number of steps necessary to accomplish an objective.

In practice, organizations accumulate complexity over time. Processes gain steps. Approval chains grow longer. Metrics multiply. Each addition may have seemed reasonable when it was introduced, but the cumulative effect is systems so complex that people work around them rather than through them.

Keep It Simple and Practical is an ongoing discipline, not a one-time cleanup. Every practice review should include questions like: Is every step in this workflow necessary? Does this metric influence behavior or just consume time to produce? Would a stakeholder see value in this output if we explained what it cost to produce?

Principle 7 is Optimize and Automate. Before an activity can be effectively automated, it needs to be optimized — that means removing waste and unnecessary complexity first, then automating the lean version. Automating a broken process just makes the process fail faster.

ITIL 4 recommends defining the appropriate level of human judgment for each activity and automating everything that does not genuinely require it. Routine, repetitive, rule-based tasks are strong automation candidates. Complex judgment calls, novel situations, and relationship-dependent interactions benefit from human involvement.

For the exam: when a scenario describes eliminating unnecessary process steps, challenging the value of existing activities, or designing a process for efficiency, Keep It Simple and Practical applies. When a scenario specifically describes automating repetitive tasks, Optimize and Automate applies.

## [16:30 – 19:30] How the Principles Interact

The Guiding Principles are not independent. They work together and reinforce each other. Let us walk through a scenario to see this in action.

An IT department is implementing ITIL 4 for the first time. They have existing processes, tools, and people. Here is how the principles guide the implementation:

Start Where You Are — assess what currently exists. Do not assume everything must be replaced.

Focus on Value — identify which existing activities contribute to stakeholder value and which do not.

Progress Iteratively with Feedback — do not try to implement all 34 practices at once. Start with the highest-value practices, implement them, gather feedback, and build from there.

Collaborate and Promote Visibility — involve the people who will use the practices in designing them. Share progress openly. Do not let the implementation become a black-box project run by a few people.

Think and Work Holistically — as you change practices, consider how each change affects other practices and other teams.

Keep It Simple and Practical — resist the temptation to design maximally comprehensive processes. Start with the minimum necessary to achieve the objective.

Optimize and Automate — as the processes stabilize, identify which steps can be automated to free staff for higher-value work.

Notice how the principles layer on top of each other. In real implementations, you will apply multiple principles simultaneously. The Foundation exam sometimes presents a scenario with multiple potentially applicable principles and asks you to identify the most relevant one. Practice that discrimination.

## [19:30 – 22:00] Principles in Practice: Two Contrasting Organizations

Let me give you two contrasting examples to anchor your intuition for the exam.

Organization A is implementing a new self-service password reset tool. The IT director says: "We should build this tool from scratch so it perfectly fits our processes." The team spends eight months building a custom tool, but users find it confusing because the design was done entirely by IT without user testing. The tool is launched, gets poor adoption, and is eventually abandoned.

Which principles were violated? Focus on Value — the goal was to enable users to reset their own passwords, but the design process lost sight of user needs. Start Where You Are — the organization ignored available commercial tools. Progress Iteratively with Feedback — the team spent eight months building without user feedback. Collaborate and Promote Visibility — users were not involved in the design.

Organization B is doing the same thing differently. They assess three commercial tools against their requirements (Start Where You Are). They pilot one tool with a team of 20 users for one month, gather feedback, and adjust the configuration (Progress Iteratively with Feedback). They involve the help desk team and a user group in the design (Collaborate and Promote Visibility). They launch the simplest version that meets the core need and add features iteratively (Keep It Simple and Practical).

Same objective. Dramatically different outcomes. The Guiding Principles are the difference.

Let us recap Module 04.

The seven ITIL 4 Guiding Principles are universal recommendations that apply to all organizational decisions and actions in all circumstances.

The seven principles are: Focus on Value; Start Where You Are; Progress Iteratively with Feedback; Collaborate and Promote Visibility; Think and Work Holistically; Keep It Simple and Practical; and Optimize and Automate.

The principles work together. Real implementations apply multiple principles simultaneously. The exam will ask you to identify which principle applies in a given scenario.
