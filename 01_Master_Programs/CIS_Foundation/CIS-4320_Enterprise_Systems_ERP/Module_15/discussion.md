## Scenario 1: The Go-Live That Almost Wasn't

A national insurance company completed its SAP S/4HANA implementation on schedule. The Go/No-Go meeting was held four days before the planned go-live date. The project manager presented the following status:

- Testing completion: 94% (target was 95%)
- Open critical defects: 2 (target was 0)
- Training completion: 71% of users trained (target was 85%)
- Data migration dress rehearsal: completed; all records migrated successfully; migration takes 11.5 hours (cutover window is 14 hours)
- Open items: 47 medium and low priority items

The executive sponsor pushed for go-live as planned, citing competitive pressure. The implementation partner project manager said "we can manage the risks."

Address the following in your post:

- Evaluate each Go/No-Go criterion against the target. Which gaps are most concerning, and why?
- What could go wrong if the company goes live with 29% of users untrained? Give a specific operational example.
- What would you recommend to the executive sponsor — go live as planned, delay, or proceed with conditions? Justify your recommendation with specific reasoning.
- What does "proceed with conditions" mean in the context of an ERP go-live, and what would those conditions look like for this scenario?

Your response should demonstrate understanding of Go/No-Go criteria, cutover risk, and how implementation methodology helps manage go-live decisions.

## Scenario 2: The Change That Didn't Stick

A regional retail chain implemented Salesforce Service Cloud eight months ago. The system was technically successful — it passed all testing, went live on time, and most users were trained. However, eight months later:

- 60% of customer service cases are still being tracked in the old Excel spreadsheets, not in Salesforce
- The new automated case routing feature is unused because reps "just handle it themselves"
- Managers are still emailing weekly status reports instead of pulling them from Salesforce dashboards
- Three of the eight service managers have resigned and cited "constant system problems" as a contributing factor

The Salesforce system is working correctly. The problem is adoption.

Address the following in your post:

- Diagnose the adoption failure using the ADKAR model. Which stages are most clearly failing, and what evidence from the scenario supports your diagnosis?
- Why does a technically successful implementation not guarantee business success?
- What three specific interventions, applied now — eight months post-go-live — could begin to reverse the adoption failure?
- Who in the organization bears the most responsibility for this outcome — the project team, the executive sponsor, or the middle managers — and why?

Your response should demonstrate understanding of the ADKAR model, change management, and post-implementation adoption challenges.

## Scenario 3: The Scope Creep Spiral

Momentum Technologies is six months into a twelve-month Salesforce Sales Cloud implementation. The original scope was: Opportunity management, Account management, Contact management, and a basic dashboard.

Since the project began, the following additions have been approved by various stakeholders:

- Week 3: VP of Marketing requested Salesforce Campaigns be added (2 weeks of additional work)
- Month 2: CFO requested custom financial reporting objects (3 weeks)
- Month 3: Sales Director requested a CPQ (Configure Price Quote) integration (6 weeks)
- Month 4: Customer Service Director requested basic Case management "since we're already in Salesforce" (4 weeks)
- Month 5: IT Director requested a full SSO integration with the company's identity provider (3 weeks)

The cumulative additions represent 18 weeks of additional work. The project is now running 14 weeks behind schedule, the budget is overrun by 35%, and the original go-live date has been pushed back twice.

Address the following in your post:

- What process failure allowed scope creep to occur five times over six months?
- Describe a formal change control process that would have evaluated each request fairly without automatically approving all of them.
- At the current point, what options does the project have to recover, and what are the trade-offs of each option?
- What is one scope addition from the list that arguably should have been approved, and one that should have been deferred, and why?

Your response should demonstrate understanding of scope management, the Iron Triangle, change control processes, and project recovery options.

## Sample Strong Opening Lines (to inspire, not copy)

For Scenario 1: "The training completion rate — 71% when the target is 85% — is actually the most concerning gap on this status sheet, because the other gaps can be managed with workarounds, but untrained users will create data quality problems from the moment the system goes live that compound over time and may never be fully cleaned up..."

For Scenario 2: "The continued use of Excel spreadsheets eight months after go-live is not a user behavior problem — it is an organizational permission problem. As long as managers accept Excel reports and do not require Salesforce data, users have a rational choice: use the tool they know, not the tool the project team wanted them to use..."

For Scenario 3: "The CPQ integration request in Month 3 is the decision that should have triggered a formal project reset, not just a change control evaluation. Six weeks of additional work on a twelve-month project is a 12.5% scope expansion in a single request — that is not an enhancement, it is a scope change that required an updated business case..."
