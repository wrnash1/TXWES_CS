## Instructions

Choose one of the three scenarios below (A, B, or C). Write an initial post responding to all three sub-questions within your chosen scenario. Then respond to at least two classmates who chose different scenarios whenever possible.

Initial post length: 175–225 words. Peer responses: at least 75 words each, adding substance beyond agreement.

## Scenario A — The Approval Gate Sprint Review

FinServ Inc. runs two-week Sprints. At the end of each Sprint, the team presents a polished 20-slide deck to the VP of Product, the Director of Engineering, and legal counsel. The VP reviews each feature and either approves it for inclusion in the product or sends it back for rework. The Scrum Master calls this the "Sprint Review." No actual code is demonstrated. Developers are not invited. The event runs 90 minutes.

Sub-questions:

1. List three specific ways this event fails to meet the Scrum Guide's definition of a Sprint Review. Use the Scrum Guide's language in your answer.
2. What is the organizational consequence of running Sprint Reviews as approval gates? How does this change the team's behavior in ways that reduce product value?
3. Design a restructured Sprint Review for FinServ Inc. that addresses the leadership's need for visibility and approval without converting the event into a gate. What would you keep, what would you remove, and what would you add?

## Scenario B — Groundhog Day Retrospectives

The Horizon Team has been running Sprint Retrospectives faithfully every Sprint for eight months. In every Retrospective, the same three issues come up: unclear requirements at Sprint Planning, unplanned work injected mid-Sprint by the VP of Engineering, and insufficient time for code review. The team writes the issues on sticky notes, the Scrum Master photographs them, and the meeting ends. In Sprint 9, the same three issues appear again. A Developer says: "What's the point of these Retrospectives? Nothing ever changes."

Sub-questions:

1. What is the Scrum Guide's requirement about what the Retrospective should produce? How is the Horizon Team's Retrospective failing to produce it?
2. The three recurring issues involve different root causes — some are internal to the team, some are organizational. How should the Scrum Master approach issues that the team cannot solve on their own?
3. Propose a specific improvement plan for one of the three recurring issues. Include: the improvement action, who is responsible, and a measurable indicator of success.

## Scenario C — The Vulnerable Retrospective

You are a new Scrum Master at a company where the engineering culture is highly competitive. Developers rarely admit when they are stuck because being stuck is seen as weakness. The existing Retrospective format asks team members to share what "didn't go well" — but no one ever says anything negative. The Scrum Master before you noted in their handoff document: "Retrospectives are pointless here — developers won't be honest."

Sub-questions:

1. What Scrum value is most at risk in this team culture, and how does its absence undermine the Retrospective's purpose?
2. What facilitation techniques or format changes could help create enough psychological safety for honest retrospective participation? Describe at least two specific approaches.
3. Is the previous Scrum Master's conclusion — "Retrospectives are pointless here" — a valid response to the situation? What does the Scrum Guide's expectations for the Scrum Master's accountability suggest about this conclusion?

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

The Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective are Scrum's primary learning mechanisms. When they are run well, teams improve every Sprint — both what they build and how they build it. When they are run as bureaucratic formalities, teams stagnate. The difference between a Scrum team that improves continuously and one that plateaus is almost always found in how seriously they take these two closing events. Be the kind of practitioner who makes these events count.
