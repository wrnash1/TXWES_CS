## Background

Every A+ technician handles hardware. Unlike software errors that can be rolled back, a single ESD event or a skipped safety step can permanently destroy a component worth hundreds of dollars. The safety procedures in this module are not bureaucratic formality — they are the difference between a repair that succeeds and one that silently introduces a new failure. This discussion asks you to apply Module 01 concepts to realistic scenarios and to explain your reasoning clearly. Clear technical reasoning is also an A+ exam skill: many Domain 3 scenario questions require you to eliminate wrong answers by understanding the why, not just the what.

### Scenario A — ESD Failure Analysis

ESD damage is invisible, cumulative, and permanent. This scenario asks you to analyze a realistic hardware failure and connect it to the physics of electrostatic discharge.

In 175–225 words, respond to the following:

- A technician at a help desk receives a desktop PC that "worked fine yesterday" but now fails to POST. The technician opens the case and notices the RAM was recently replaced — there is no ESD mat on the desk and no wrist strap in sight. What is the most likely cause of the failure, and how would you confirm it? Be specific: what test would you perform, what would a positive result look like, and what would a negative result tell you?
- The A+ exam presents this distractor: "The ESD wrist strap protects the technician from electric shock." Explain precisely why this statement is wrong. What does the wrist strap actually protect, how does the 1-megaohm resistor work, and what property of static electricity makes 10 volts dangerous to a chip when 3,000 volts is needed before a human feels anything?
- If you were setting up a hardware repair station for a small IT department, what three ESD protection measures would you implement and why?

### Scenario B — Safety Procedure Justification

The A+ exam tests procedure order — not just what to do, but why each step must happen in the sequence it does. This scenario asks you to articulate the rationale for each step.

In 175–225 words, respond to the following:

- A new IT intern argues: "I just turn off the power strip when I work on PCs. That cuts the power — what's the difference?" Write a technical explanation for the intern that covers: what voltage remains present after the strip is turned off but the cord is still plugged in, why the PSU capacitors matter, and what the correct procedure is instead.
- Explain the purpose of Step 3 in the safety procedure: pressing the power button after unplugging the cord. What specific component does this step discharge, and why can skipping it cause a problem even after the cord is physically removed?
- A technician clips their ESD wrist strap to the painted exterior of the PC case. Why does this not work? What physical property of paint makes it unsuitable as a grounding point, and where should the strap be clipped instead?

### Scenario C — Component Identification and Diagnosis

Knowing what each component does and where it is physically located is the foundation of every hardware diagnostic. This scenario applies that knowledge to A+ style scenario questions.

In 175–225 words, respond to the following:

- A user reports: "I built a new PC last night. It powers on — I can hear the fans and see lights — but my monitor shows no signal." List the top three hardware-related causes you would check, in the order you would check them. For each cause, name the specific component, explain why it produces this symptom, and describe the physical action you would take to verify or resolve it.
- Describe the physical differences between DDR4 and DDR5 RAM that prevent accidental cross-installation. Why does this matter for a technician who is purchasing an upgrade for an existing system?
- A colleague tells you: "I can save money by buying a PGA processor for this LGA motherboard — they're both socket AM5." Explain why this statement contains a fundamental error and what damage could result from attempting the installation.

## A Note from Professor Nash

The habits you build in Module 01 follow you for the rest of your career. Every time you pick up a component without an ESD strap, you are gambling. Most of the time you win — the discharge is small, the component survives. But ESD damage is cumulative. A chip that has absorbed five near-invisible discharges is not the same chip it was. It may work for weeks or months before failing in a way that looks like a random malfunction. The technician who skipped the wrist strap will never connect the dots. The technician who always uses the strap never has to.

The A+ exam tests these procedures because CompTIA knows that the difference between a competent technician and a destructive one is often not knowledge of advanced topics — it is discipline about the basics. Learn them correctly once. Apply them automatically every time.
