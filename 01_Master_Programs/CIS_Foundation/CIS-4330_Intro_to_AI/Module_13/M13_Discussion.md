## Scenario A — The ROI Skeptic

A regional grocery chain's CFO reviewed an AI demand forecasting proposal from the data science team. The team projected $4.2M in annual savings from reduced overstock and fewer stockouts. The CFO dismissed the proposal, saying: "You built a financial model on top of another model. Every number you gave me is an assumption, not a fact. We could spend $800K on something with no proven return."

**Prompt:** How should the data science team respond to the CFO's skepticism? Is the CFO wrong, or does she have legitimate concerns about how AI ROI is typically estimated? What evidence, framing, or methodology changes would make the business case more convincing? Apply the ROI framework from Module 13 in your response.

## Scenario B — AI in Healthcare Liability

A rural hospital has deployed an AI model that predicts which emergency department patients are at risk of sepsis. The model flags high-risk patients for immediate physician review. One night, the model does not flag a patient who later develops severe sepsis. The family's attorney argues the hospital was negligent for relying on an AI system that failed to alert physicians.

**Prompt:** Who bears responsibility in this scenario — the hospital, the AI vendor, the physician, or some combination? How should healthcare organizations frame AI as "clinical decision support" rather than "clinical decision making," and why does that distinction matter legally and ethically? What safeguards should the hospital have had in place?

## Scenario C — Retail Personalization and Privacy

A major online retailer is considering deploying an AI recommendation engine that incorporates not just purchase history but also location data, social media activity (from a data broker), and in-store foot traffic patterns captured by cameras. The personalization improvement is estimated to increase revenue by $120M annually. The privacy team raises concerns that customers have not consented to social media data use.

**Prompt:** Should the retailer deploy this recommendation system as described? Where is the ethical line between personalization that customers value and surveillance-based targeting that violates their reasonable expectations? What changes to the data inputs or consent model would make this initiative ethically sound? Apply the ROI framework — does the financial case change if the social media data is excluded?

## Instructor Modeling Response — Scenario C Sample

*The following is a model response at the "Excellent" level to help calibrate your writing.*

The retailer is correct that a $120M revenue opportunity is significant, but the inclusion of social media data acquired via a data broker without explicit customer consent is the pivot point of this scenario. Under CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act) and similar state laws, customers have the right to know what data is collected about them and to opt out of its sale or use for targeted advertising. If this retailer serves California customers — and virtually all major retailers do — the social media data layer likely creates compliance exposure that the $120M figure does not account for.

Applying the ROI framework rigorously requires the team to estimate the probability and cost of regulatory penalties, class action risk, and reputational harm in the total cost of ownership column. Those are real costs, not hypothetical.

More importantly, the ethical question is not what is technically legal but what the customer would reasonably expect. Most shoppers do not expect their social media activity — posted for friends and family — to be fed into a commercial recommendation engine. That gap between expectation and reality, when exposed, creates brand damage that erodes the very customer loyalty the personalization was supposed to build.

The fix is straightforward: use purchase history and in-store data (with clear signage), exclude the social media feed, and offer an explicit opt-in for customers who want maximum personalization. The revenue impact of removing one data source is likely modest; the legal and reputational risk reduction is substantial.
