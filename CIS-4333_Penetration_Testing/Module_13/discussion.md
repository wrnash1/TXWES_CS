## Scenario A: The Inflated Report

A penetration tester submits a draft report to their project lead. The report contains 47 findings — but reviewing them, the project lead notices that 15 are duplicates of the same misconfiguration across different systems (HTTP instead of HTTPS on internal servers), 10 are "informational" findings with no security impact, and 8 are theoretical vulnerabilities that were not confirmed during testing. The client is a mid-sized manufacturer that expected a 20-page report; this draft is 95 pages.

Address the following: What is the problem with duplicating the same finding 15 times? How should a tester handle the same finding affecting multiple systems? What is the appropriate standard for including unconfirmed findings in a report? How does report length affect the report's value, and what guidance would you give the tester to bring this draft to a professional standard?

---

## Scenario B: The Missing Evidence

Your firm delivered a penetration test report to a law firm six months ago. The client is now disputing a Critical finding that claimed their document management system was vulnerable to unauthenticated remote file access. The client's IT team says they cannot reproduce the vulnerability and believes the tester made an error. When you check the tester's notes and evidence archive, you find the screenshot was deleted "to save disk space" and the testing laptop was wiped.

Address the following: What professional and legal obligations are at risk in this situation? What evidence archiving policy would have prevented this problem — be specific about retention period, storage format, and access controls? How should the firm respond to the client's dispute given the absence of evidence? What should the firm implement to ensure this situation does not recur?

---

## Scenario C: The Scope Creep Finding

During a penetration test of a healthcare company's corporate IT infrastructure, the tester discovers a misconfigured server that appears to host patient health records — clearly a HIPAA-covered system. The server was not included in the authorized scope. The tester investigates briefly and confirms the server stores unencrypted PHI for approximately 4,000 patients.

Address the following: The server was not in scope — should this finding appear in the report? What is the tester's obligation under HIPAA when they encounter PHI, even if the discovery was incidental? What is the immediate action the tester must take, and when? How would you write the finding in the report — what language, what risk rating, and what caveat about scope would you include? What does this scenario illustrate about the limitations of narrowly scoped assessments?

A strong peer response does more than agree. Consider:
