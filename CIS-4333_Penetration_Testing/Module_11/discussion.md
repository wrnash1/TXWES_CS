## Scenario A: The Training Failure

Six months ago, your firm conducted a baseline phishing campaign for a mid-sized financial services company. The click rate was 38% and the credential submission rate was 22%. The company invested in a mandatory phishing awareness training program. You have just completed the follow-up campaign and the click rate is 34% and the submission rate is 20%. The CISO is disappointed. The VP of HR believes the test was unfair because the second phishing template was "more sophisticated" than the first.

Address the following: How would you evaluate whether the training had an effect? What statistical or contextual factors must be controlled when comparing two campaign results? Is the VP of HR's complaint valid, and how would you address it? What three specific changes to the awareness program would you recommend to achieve measurable improvement in the next 6 months?

## Scenario B: The Ethical Line

A client has authorized a comprehensive social engineering assessment including email phishing, vishing, and "any technique necessary to gain unauthorized access." During planning, your project lead suggests using a pretext based on a recent company layoff — telling employees they are being called to schedule their termination interview, which will push them to comply with requests quickly to avoid angering their supervisor.

Address the following: Is this pretext within the bounds of ethical social engineering testing? Explain your reasoning with reference to specific ethical principles. Would you refuse this technique even if the client has explicitly authorized it? What alternative pretext could achieve similar urgency pressure without exploiting layoff fear? How would you document this decision in the engagement record?

---

## Scenario C: The Insider Threat Test

A retail company asks you to test for insider threat susceptibility. The approved scope includes testing whether employees would share credentials, provide access codes, or send sensitive files in response to requests from unknown parties posing as management. During the test, you call an entry-level employee, impersonate the Regional Director, and successfully get her to email you a spreadsheet containing customer credit card data (masked test data, not real numbers). She then calls her manager, visibly upset, believing she has accidentally sent sensitive data to the wrong person.

Address the following: How do you evaluate the test result — was this a success or a failure of the test design? What is your immediate obligation when you learn the employee is distressed? How would this scenario influence the way you design future insider threat tests? What changes to the scope document and abort protocol would prevent this outcome?
