## Scenario A: The Skeptical Client

You have just delivered a physical penetration test report to Hartwell Manufacturing. Your most critical finding is that all five of the company's RFID access control readers use 125 kHz HID Prox cards. During the test, you cloned a contractor's card in a shared hallway and used it to access the server room, the executive floor, and the payroll office. The company's VP of Operations argues that the card cloning required specialized expensive equipment that "a real hacker wouldn't bother with" and wants to downgrade the finding to Medium.

Address the following: How do you respond to the VP's argument about specialized equipment? Is the argument valid in the current threat landscape? What data or evidence would you present to support your original High/Critical rating? Draft a three-sentence response to include in the report's management response section that professionally rebuts this assessment.

---

## Scenario B: The Unexpected Discovery

During an authorized physical penetration test of a law firm, you successfully tailgate into the building and make your way to an unoccupied conference room. On the conference room table, you find a printed spreadsheet left face-down. When you turn it over to assess the sensitivity of the unsecured document, you discover it contains names, Social Security numbers, and legal case identifiers for approximately 200 individuals.

Address the following: What is your immediate legal obligation regarding this discovery? What must you do before continuing with any other test activities? How does this discovery change the risk profile of the "unattended conference room" finding? What specific language in the scope of work would have pre-defined the protocol for this situation, and how would you include it in future contracts?

---

## Scenario C: The Third-Party Complication

You are conducting a physical penetration test of Nexum Corp, which occupies floors 8–12 of a 20-story office building. The authorized scope covers all areas of the building operated by Nexum. During testing, you observe that the building's ground-floor lobby has no access control — any member of the public can enter and take elevators to any floor. When you reach Floor 8, a Nexum employee holds the secured elevator lobby door open for you after you make brief eye contact.

Address the following: The lobby is managed by the building owner, not Nexum. Can you include the lack of lobby access control in your report, and if so, how? Classify the tailgating incident — does it represent a Nexum security failure, a building management failure, or both? What recommendation would you make to Nexum that addresses the vulnerability without requiring them to control third-party property? How does this scenario illustrate the concept of "inherited risk" in physical security assessments?

## Peer Response Guidance

A strong peer response does more than agree. Consider:

- Extending the analysis to address a different stakeholder perspective (insurance, legal, regulatory)
