**Scenario A — The Analogy Challenge**

In the video lecture, Professor Nash used a cooking analogy to explain the difference between compiled and interpreted languages: a compiled language is like hiring a translator in advance who hands you a completed English recipe, while an interpreted language is like having the translator right there in the kitchen translating live.

In 175–225 words, write your **own original analogy** that explains the difference between compiled and interpreted languages — one that is different from the cooking example. Your analogy should make it clear:

- What the "source code" represents in your analogy
- What the "compilation" step represents
- What the "interpretation" (line-by-line execution) represents
- One practical trade-off between the two approaches (speed, portability, flexibility, etc.)

Bonus: Extend your analogy to include Python's bytecode step — where does `.pyc` fit in?

---

**Scenario B — REPL vs. Script: When Would You Use Each?**

You now have hands-on experience with both the Python REPL and script mode. In 175–225 words, answer the following:

- Describe a real-world software task where the REPL would be the *right* tool to use. Be specific — what would you be doing, and why is the REPL better than writing a script for that situation?
- Describe a different real-world software task where script mode is clearly the *right* choice. Again, be specific.
- Explain the one behavior difference between REPL and script mode that surprised you most when you first encountered it during the lab.

---

**Scenario C — The Indentation Debate**

Python's mandatory indentation is one of the most controversial design choices in any major programming language. Developers who come from C, Java, or JavaScript often find it jarring at first. Some argue it is one of Python's greatest strengths; others argue it is an unnecessary restriction.

In 175–225 words:

- Argue **in favor** of Python's mandatory indentation — make the strongest case you can for why it is a good language design decision.
- Then briefly acknowledge **one legitimate criticism** of mandatory indentation (there are real ones).
- Based on your lab experience writing `indentation_demo.py` and the intentional `broken_indent.py`, do you personally find indentation helpful or annoying at this point? Be honest — there is no wrong answer.

## Tips for a Strong Post

- **Be specific.** Vague statements like "Python is useful in many ways" earn partial credit. Specific statements like "The REPL is ideal when you're exploring an unfamiliar API method and want to test its return value before committing it to a script" earn full credit.
- **Use your lab experience.** You saw an `IndentationError` firsthand. You saw that bare expressions in a script produce no output. Reference those moments.
- **It's okay to have an opinion.** Scenario C asks for your personal reaction. Being honest and thoughtful earns more credit than a vague, non-committal answer.
- **Technical vocabulary matters.** Use terms like REPL, bytecode, PVM, CPython, interpreted, compiled, script mode, `IndentationError` — and use them correctly.
