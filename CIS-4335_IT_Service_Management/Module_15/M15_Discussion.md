## Scenario A: The Change Freeze Conflict

A fintech company's development team is in the middle of a two-week sprint and has two high-priority features ready to deploy. However, the ITSM team has declared a change freeze for the next 10 days because the company is approaching fiscal year-end — a critical period for financial system stability. The development team argues that their features affect only the consumer-facing mobile app, not the financial processing backend, and that the sprint commitment to the business owner requires delivery this week.

**Prompt:** How should the organization handle this conflict? Is a blanket change freeze ever compatible with continuous delivery practices? What is the ITIL 4-aligned way to design change freeze policies that distinguish high-risk changes (financial systems near fiscal close) from low-risk changes (consumer UI features)? Apply at least one ITIL Guiding Principle and one DevOps concept to your recommendation.

## Scenario B: "We Don't Need ITIL, We Have DevOps"

A startup has scaled from 10 to 150 engineers over three years. Their DevOps practices are strong: CI/CD pipelines, Kubernetes, observability tooling, daily deployments. However, they have had three major incidents in the past six months where:

- A developer deployed a breaking API change without notifying downstream teams.
- A critical database migration was executed without a tested rollback plan.
- An on-call engineer could not determine which of 47 recent deployments caused a production anomaly.

The CTO says "we don't need ITIL — we have DevOps." Senior engineers are pushing back, saying some governance is needed.

**Prompt:** Using the three incidents as specific evidence, explain what ITIL practices would have prevented or mitigated each. Argue for a lightweight, DevOps-compatible ITIL implementation rather than a heavyweight traditional one. Which ITIL Guiding Principle best summarizes how to introduce governance without destroying the startup's culture?

## Scenario C: The Human Cost of Toil

A cloud operations team of 8 engineers manages infrastructure supporting 200 development teams. The ops team handles 300–400 operational tasks per week: password resets, certificate renewals, access provisioning, routine restarts of unstable services, and manual compliance report generation. The team works long hours and turnover is high — three engineers left in the past year. The remaining engineers describe their job as "purely reactive, never creative."

**Prompt:** Using the SRE concept of toil, categorize the described work into toil vs. non-toil. Estimate what percentage of the team's effort is toil. What is the human cost of excessive toil, and how does it connect to turnover? Design a 6-month toil reduction roadmap using automation and process improvement. How does this align with ITIL 4's "optimize and automate" principle?
