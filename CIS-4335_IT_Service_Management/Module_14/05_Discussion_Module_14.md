## Overview

Risk and compliance decisions in IT are rarely black and white. They require judgment about how to balance cost, operational impact, regulatory obligation, and risk tolerance. The scenarios below present realistic situations where risk and compliance concepts must be applied thoughtfully. Responses that simply restate definitions will not receive full credit — focus on applying the concepts to the specific context.

---

## Scenario 1: The Risk Acceptance Decision

A regional bank's IT risk committee is reviewing its risk register. One risk entry reads: "The bank's online banking platform runs on a version of the Java runtime that reached end-of-vendor-support four months ago. No critical vulnerabilities have been publicly disclosed yet, but security researchers have announced ongoing research into the platform. The upgrade project has been estimated at six months and $340,000." The CTO is arguing for accepting this risk because "nothing bad has happened yet." The CISO is arguing for mitigating the risk by accelerating the upgrade. The CFO wants a formal risk acceptance decision documented before the next board meeting.

**Initial Post Prompt (Due Wednesday at 11:59 PM):**

In 175–225 words, respond to the following:

- Evaluate the CTO's "nothing bad has happened yet" rationale using ITIL 4 risk management principles — is this a sound basis for risk acceptance?
- What specific information would need to be included in a formal risk acceptance decision to make it defensible to the bank's board and potential regulators?
- If you were the CISO, what would you argue is the most important reason to mitigate rather than accept this particular risk?

**Peer Response Prompt (Due Sunday at 11:59 PM):**

Respond to at least two classmates' posts (minimum 75 words each):

- Challenge or affirm their evaluation of the CTO's rationale with a specific argument
- Add one regulatory consideration relevant to a bank that they may not have included
- Discuss whether the $340,000 upgrade cost changes the risk calculus, and how

## Scenario 2: The SOC 2 Audit Preparation Sprint

A SaaS company has been notified that its largest client — representing 38% of annual revenue — will require a SOC 2 Type II report within 90 days as a condition of contract renewal. The company has never undergone a SOC 2 audit. An internal assessment reveals: no formal change management process (changes are deployed by developers directly to production), no documented incident response plan, and no access review process — former employees' accounts are reviewed manually and inconsistently. The CEO says, "We just need to pass the audit. Do whatever it takes in 90 days."

**Initial Post Prompt (Due Wednesday at 11:59 PM):**

In 175–225 words, respond to the following:

- Identify the two highest-priority gaps from the assessment and explain why they are highest priority for a SOC 2 Security criterion audit specifically.
- Is it realistic to achieve a SOC 2 Type II report in 90 days given the identified gaps? Explain your answer using the difference between Type I and Type II.
- What honest advice would you give the CEO about what is achievable in 90 days and what the path to a full Type II report actually looks like?

**Peer Response Prompt (Due Sunday at 11:59 PM):**

Respond to at least two classmates' posts (minimum 75 words each):

- Evaluate whether their gap prioritization aligns with the SOC 2 Security criterion — do you agree with their top two?
- Add one practical step for achieving the Type I milestone within the 90-day window
- Discuss the business risk of losing 38% of revenue versus the cost and disruption of the compliance sprint

## Scenario 3: The Compliance Dashboard Debate

An IT director at a government contractor presents a compliance dashboard at a quarterly leadership meeting. The dashboard shows: 94% of controls green, 6% yellow, zero red. The compliance team celebrates. Two weeks later, an external auditor reports 17 findings from the same quarter — including three related to access control failures that affect systems handling government contract data. The IT director is confused: "How can we have a 94% green dashboard and 17 audit findings?"

**Initial Post Prompt (Due Wednesday at 11:59 PM):**

In 175–225 words, respond to the following:

- Explain three specific ways a compliance dashboard can show high green percentages while significant compliance failures exist.
- What does this scenario suggest about the quality of the evidence being used to populate the dashboard — and specifically about the difference between policy compliance and operational compliance?
- What would you redesign in the dashboard to catch the type of access control failures the auditor found?

**Peer Response Prompt (Due Sunday at 11:59 PM):**

Respond to at least two classmates' posts (minimum 75 words each):

- Add one specific dashboard design change they did not mention
- Discuss whether the problem is the dashboard itself or the underlying control testing methodology
- Evaluate whether government contractor compliance obligations (such as CMMC or DFARS) would change the dashboard requirements and how
