## Purpose of This Guide

## 1. What Are the Guiding Principles?

The Guiding Principles are recommendations that guide an organization in all circumstances, regardless of changes in its goals, strategies, type of work, or management structure. ITIL 4 describes them as universal because:

* They apply at every organizational level — from strategic leadership to front-line staff.
* They apply in every situation — no practice, project, or decision is exempt.
* They are not rules with penalties for violation — they are best-practice recommendations.
* They can be applied independently or in combination, as relevant to a given situation.

The exam tests your ability to identify which principle is most relevant in a scenario and why.

## 2. The Seven Guiding Principles — Complete Reference

### Principle 1: Focus on Value

Everything the organization does should directly or indirectly create value for the organization, its customers, and other stakeholders. Value must be understood from the stakeholder's perspective — what the service provider considers valuable may differ from what the customer actually needs.

Practical implications:

* Before starting any work, ask: how does this contribute to stakeholder value?
* When designing services, keep the consumer's outcomes in mind, not just technical deliverables.
* When reviewing existing activities, ask which ones genuinely contribute to value and which can be eliminated.

Exam trigger phrases: "oriented around customer outcomes," "eliminating activities that don't contribute," "ensuring work traces back to business needs."

---

### Principle 2: Start Where You Are

Do not start from scratch and build something new without first considering what is already available to be leveraged. Assess the current state honestly before designing a new state.

Practical implications:

* Measure and observe existing processes before redesigning them.
* Identify what is working and build on it rather than discarding it.
* Use existing data, tools, and knowledge as inputs to improvement planning.

Exam trigger phrases: "assessing current state," "building on existing capabilities," "not replacing functional tools unnecessarily," "conducting a baseline assessment."

---

### Principle 3: Progress Iteratively with Feedback

Organize work into smaller, manageable sections that can be executed and completed in a timely manner. Do not attempt to do everything at once. Seek feedback before, during, and after each iteration.

Practical implications:

* Pilot new practices in one area before organization-wide rollout.
* Deliver in increments and gather feedback after each one.
* Use feedback to adjust the next iteration before committing further resources.

Exam trigger phrases: "piloting before scaling," "incremental delivery," "gathering feedback," "adjusting based on results," "small steps with review checkpoints."

---

### Principle 4: Collaborate and Promote Visibility

Inclusion is generally better than exclusion. Work across silos. Make information available to the people who need it. Involve the right stakeholders in decisions.

Practical implications:

* Involve users, customers, and operational staff in service and practice design.
* Share work-in-progress transparently rather than presenting only finished results.
* Break down team silos that prevent information from reaching where it is needed.

Exam trigger phrases: "involving stakeholders," "breaking down silos," "sharing information openly," "cross-functional participation," "making work visible."

---

### Principle 5: Think and Work Holistically

No service, practice, process, department, or supplier stands alone. All elements of the service management environment work together in an integrated system. A change to one component affects others.

Practical implications:

* When redesigning a practice, assess the impact on connected practices, teams, and tools.
* Consider all four dimensions when making service decisions.
* Resist optimizing one component in isolation if it degrades the whole system.

Exam trigger phrases: "considering downstream effects," "integrated system thinking," "impacts on connected practices," "end-to-end view."

---

### Principle 6: Keep It Simple and Practical

If a process, service, action, or metric fails to provide value or produce a useful outcome, eliminate it. Use the minimum number of steps necessary to accomplish an objective.

Practical implications:

* Regularly review processes and metrics to identify unnecessary complexity.
* Challenge every approval step, report, and procedure: does this genuinely add value?
* Design for the common case, not the rare exception.

Exam trigger phrases: "eliminating unnecessary steps," "reducing complexity," "streamlining workflows," "challenging the value of existing activities."

---

### Principle 7: Optimize and Automate

Optimize processes before automating them. Then automate everything that does not genuinely require human judgment. Use human attention where it adds the most value.

Practical implications:

* Do not automate broken processes — optimize first, then automate.
* Identify repetitive, rule-based tasks as automation candidates.
* Retain human judgment for novel situations, complex decisions, and relationship-dependent interactions.

Exam trigger phrases: "automating repetitive tasks," "reducing manual effort," "freeing staff for higher-value work," "optimizing before automating."

## 4. Principles in Combination: Real Scenario Walk-Through

An organization is replacing its ticketing system for the first time in eight years. Here is how multiple principles apply simultaneously:

Start Where You Are — before selecting a new tool, audit what the current tool does well and where it fails. Export and review the historical data before committing to a replacement platform.

Focus on Value — clarify what the ticketing system is supposed to enable: faster incident resolution, better visibility into service requests, improved problem identification. Keep those outcomes as the selection criteria.

Progress Iteratively with Feedback — do not deploy the new system to all 2,000 staff on day one. Pilot with the help desk team, gather feedback on configuration and workflows, adjust, then expand.

Collaborate and Promote Visibility — involve the help desk staff who will use the system daily in the configuration decisions. Share the migration timeline and training plan openly.

Think and Work Holistically — assess how the new ticketing system integrates with the monitoring platform, the knowledge base, and the change management workflow.

Keep It Simple and Practical — resist configuring every possible field and workflow in the new system. Start with what is needed; add complexity only when a clear need emerges.

Optimize and Automate — once the core workflows are stable, automate ticket routing, acknowledgment emails, and escalation notifications.

## 5. Distinguishing Similar Principles

The exam sometimes presents two principles that seem similar. Here are the most common areas of confusion.

### Focus on Value vs. Keep It Simple and Practical

Both are about removing waste, but from different angles. Focus on Value asks "does this activity contribute to outcomes stakeholders care about?" Keep It Simple and Practical asks "is this activity more complex than it needs to be?" A useful heuristic: if the question is about whether an activity should exist, it is Focus on Value. If the question is about whether the activity is unnecessarily complex, it is Keep It Simple and Practical.

### Start Where You Are vs. Progress Iteratively with Feedback

Start Where You Are is about assessment before beginning — not discarding what works. Progress Iteratively with Feedback is about how you implement change — in increments with feedback loops. Start Where You Are happens before the first iteration; Progress Iteratively governs all subsequent iterations.

### Think and Work Holistically vs. Collaborate and Promote Visibility

Think and Work Holistically is about system-wide impact analysis — considering how a change affects connected components. Collaborate and Promote Visibility is about human communication and inclusion — involving the right people and making information available. You can think holistically in isolation; you cannot collaborate without other people.

### Keep It Simple and Practical vs. Optimize and Automate

Keep It Simple and Practical focuses on reducing unnecessary complexity in processes and activities. Optimize and Automate focuses on removing human effort from tasks that do not require judgment. The ITIL 4 guidance is clear: optimize (simplify) first, then automate. These principles have a deliberate sequence.

## 6. ITIL 4 Foundation Exam Tips

1. **Know all seven principle names cold.** You cannot recognize a principle in a scenario if you cannot name it. Memorize the names first.

2. **Each principle has a trigger pattern.** Study the exam trigger phrases in the table above. Exam scenarios will use language that maps to one of these patterns.

3. **Progress Iteratively with Feedback is about Agile-style delivery.** The principle incorporates feedback as an equal element with iteration. A question about "piloting before scaling" maps here.

4. **Start Where You Are is not about accepting the status quo.** It is about assessing honestly before changing. The principle does not say "never change"; it says "understand what you have before deciding what to change."

5. **Optimize and Automate has a sequence.** Optimize first, then automate. A scenario describing automation of a broken process violates this principle.

6. **The principles are independent.** None of the seven supersedes any other. In exam questions asking which principle is most relevant, pick the one most directly described by the scenario.

7. **Guiding Principles vs. governance policies.** Guiding Principles are universal recommendations; governance policies are specific directives established by organizational leadership. Do not confuse them.

8. **The principles apply to everyone.** The exam may describe a junior analyst making a decision. The Guiding Principles apply to that decision just as they apply to leadership decisions.

## 7. Key Terms Glossary

**Collaborate and Promote Visibility** — The Guiding Principle directing organizations to work across silos, involve stakeholders in decisions, and make information available to those who need it.

**Focus on Value** — The Guiding Principle directing that every action should directly or indirectly create value for the organization, its customers, and other stakeholders.

**Guiding Principles** — Seven universal recommendations that apply to all organizational decisions and actions regardless of circumstances.

**Keep It Simple and Practical** — The Guiding Principle directing organizations to eliminate activities and complexity that do not contribute to value.

**Optimize and Automate** — The Guiding Principle directing organizations to optimize processes before automating them, and to automate everything that does not genuinely require human judgment.

**Progress Iteratively with Feedback** — The Guiding Principle directing organizations to work in small, manageable increments and gather feedback to guide each subsequent increment.

**Start Where You Are** — The Guiding Principle directing organizations to assess and leverage existing capabilities before building new ones.

**Think and Work Holistically** — The Guiding Principle directing organizations to consider the whole system and the impacts of changes on connected components.
