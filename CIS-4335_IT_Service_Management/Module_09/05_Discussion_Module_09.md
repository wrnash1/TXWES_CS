## Scenario 1: The KEDB Nobody Uses (175–225 words)

Greenfield Insurance has maintained a Known Error Database for three years. It currently
contains 214 entries. However, a recent audit found that service desk agents almost never
consult it. When asked why, agents said: "It takes too long to search," "The entries are
out of date," and "Half the workarounds don't work anymore."

The Problem Management team blames the service desk for not using the tool. The service
desk blames Problem Management for maintaining a low-quality database.

Using ITIL 4 principles, analyze who is correct in this dispute — or whether both parties
share responsibility. What specific conditions must be true for a KEDB to deliver its
intended value? What would you recommend to the IT Service Manager to turn this from a
blame conversation into an improvement initiative? Identify at least two specific,
actionable changes — one for the Problem Management team and one for the service desk —
that would improve KEDB utilization and effectiveness.

## Scenario 2: The CAB That Slows Everything Down (175–225 words)

A software development team at a retail company has started bypassing the Change Advisory
Board. They deploy small code updates directly to production on Friday afternoons, arguing
that "the CAB adds two weeks to every release and our competitors are shipping daily."

The IT Service Manager discovers this practice after a Friday deployment causes a P1
incident that affects the online checkout system for four hours on a Saturday morning —
the company's peak sales period.

Using ITIL 4 Change Enablement principles, evaluate the development team's argument
and the IT Service Manager's position. Is there merit to the developers' complaint about
CAB speed? How does ITIL 4 account for organizations that need to deploy frequently and
quickly? What change model or change type would be most appropriate for the team's
low-risk, frequent code deployments — and what conditions must be met before that model
can be applied? Reference at least one specific ITIL 4 concept in your response.

## Scenario 3: The Problem That Was Never a Problem (175–225 words)

A large university IT department tracks the following data from the past six months:

- 312 password reset incidents
- 89 VPN connection failure incidents
- 74 "slow file shares" incidents
- 64 printer configuration incidents

Each incident type is resolved individually by the service desk. No problem records have
been raised for any of these categories, even though the same issues recur week after week.

The IT Director reviews this data and says: "We spend 80% of our incident response time
on issues we have already solved before. Something is wrong with our process."

Using ITIL 4 Problem Management concepts, explain what the IT Director is observing in
structural terms. Which specific aspect of the problem management lifecycle is absent?
What criteria should an IT organization use to decide when to raise a problem record from
a pattern of incidents — rather than treating each incident individually? Propose a
specific trigger rule (for example, "if incident type X recurs more than N times in Y
days, a problem record is automatically raised") and explain how it would change this
organization's operations.
