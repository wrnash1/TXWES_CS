## Scenario 1: The Well-Intentioned Service Desk Manager (175–225 words)

Marcus manages a service desk of 14 agents at a regional healthcare network. His team
is proud of their metrics: 96% ticket closure rate, 89% customer satisfaction score,
and an average handle time of 6 minutes.

However, during a recent audit, it was discovered that agents frequently close tickets
after applying temporary fixes without logging the underlying issue as a problem. When
asked, agents say they close tickets quickly to "keep the numbers clean." The same
issues reappear week after week.

Using ITIL 4 principles, explain what is wrong with this approach. Specifically: how
does the incentive structure around ticket closure metrics conflict with the service
management goal of minimizing incident recurrence? Which ITIL 4 practice is being
undermined? What change would you recommend Marcus make — not just to the process,
but to the metrics his team is evaluated on? Support your answer with ITIL 4 concepts
from Module 08 and any related modules.

## Scenario 2: Monitoring Without Response (175–225 words)

Priya is the IT Operations Manager at a logistics company. Her team uses an enterprise
monitoring platform that generates approximately 4,200 alerts per day. The operations
team reviews the alert dashboard every two hours. In the last quarter, the company has
experienced six P1 incidents — three of which had warning events logged in the
monitoring system 20–45 minutes before the outage occurred, but no one acted on them
because the alert dashboard was too noisy to identify what mattered.

Priya's CISO argues the solution is "more monitoring — more data, better coverage."
Priya disagrees.

Using ITIL 4 Monitoring and Event Management concepts, explain why more monitoring data
is not necessarily the solution to Priya's problem. What is the actual problem, and what
changes to the event management approach would address it? Reference at least two
specific concepts from the Module 08 reading guide or video in your response.

## Scenario 3: The P1 That Became a Lesson (175–225 words)

A financial services firm experienced a P1 incident last Thursday: their online banking
portal was unavailable for 2 hours and 17 minutes during peak morning hours. The
incident was resolved. The war room worked well — coordinated response, clear roles,
timely stakeholder communications.

However, the CTO is now asking: "Why did this happen? How do we make sure it never
happens again?"

The Incident Manager responds: "We fixed the problem. The system is back up. The
incident is closed."

The CTO is frustrated by this answer — and rightly so according to ITIL 4.

Explain the gap between what the Incident Manager said and what ITIL 4 best practice
requires. What should happen now that the incident is resolved? Which practice takes
over, and what does it produce? What is the purpose of a Post-Incident Review, and
what are its three key outputs? Why is the CTO's question — "how do we make sure it
never happens again" — not answerable by Incident Management alone?
