## Segment 2 — Communication Planning (2:30–7:00)

[SHOW SLIDE: "The Communications Formula"]

Let us start with one of the Project+ exam's most frequently tested quantitative communication concepts. When you have a project team, every pair of people is a potential communication channel. The formula for calculating the number of communication channels is `n(n-1)/2`, where n is the number of people.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

With 5 team members: `5(5-1)/2 = 10` channels. With 10 team members: `10(9)/2 = 45` channels. With 20 team members: `20(19)/2 = 190` channels. Notice how rapidly the number of channels explodes as the team grows. This is why large teams require formalized communication plans — informal communication simply cannot scale.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Adding Team Members — The Complexity Jump"]

Here is an exam-style application: a project currently has 8 team members. A new member is being added. How many new communication channels are created? Before: `8(7)/2 = 28` channels. After: `9(8)/2 = 36` channels. Difference: 36 - 28 = 8 new channels. Each new team member adds n-1 new channels, where n is the new total size.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

This is not just math trivia — it explains why adding people to a late project (Brook's Law) often makes it later. More people mean exponentially more communication overhead.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Communication Management Plan"]

The Communication Management Plan documents how project information will be planned, structured, monitored, and distributed. It answers five core questions: Who needs what information? When do they need it? In what format? Through which channel? And who is responsible for delivering it?

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

Key elements of the Communications Management Plan include:

- Stakeholder communication requirements
- Information types, formats, and detail levels for each audience
- Communication methods: push (emails, reports sent to recipients), pull (document repositories, intranet sites), and interactive (meetings, calls, collaboration platforms)
- Communication frequency and schedule
- Escalation process for communication issues
- Glossary of terminology

[SHOW SLIDE: "Push, Pull, and Interactive Communication"]

Three communication method types appear on the Project+ exam. Push communication sends information to specific recipients — they receive it whether they actively seek it or not. Examples: status reports, meeting minutes, email updates. Use push for required informational updates.

Pull communication makes information available for stakeholders to access when they choose. Examples: project SharePoint site, document repository, project dashboard. Use pull for reference materials and large files that not everyone needs immediately.

Interactive communication involves real-time, two-way exchange between two or more parties. Examples: meetings, phone calls, video conferences. Use interactive when discussion, decision-making, or problem-solving is needed.

## Segment 3 — Stakeholder Identification and Analysis (7:00–11:30)

[SHOW SLIDE: "Step 1 — Identify Stakeholders"]

Stakeholder management begins with identification. A stakeholder is any individual, group, or organization that may affect, be affected by, or perceive themselves to be affected by the project. The key word is "perceive" — you must manage stakeholders who feel impacted even if you believe they are not actually impacted.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

The Stakeholder Register is the output of the identification process. It documents for each stakeholder: name and role, organizational affiliation, contact information, interests and expectations, influence level, potential impact on the project, and engagement strategy.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Stakeholder Analysis Approaches"]

After identification, you analyze stakeholders to understand their relative importance and develop appropriate engagement strategies. The two analysis tools tested most heavily on Project+ are the Power-Interest grid and the Salience Model.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Power-Interest Grid — Four Quadrants"]

The Power-Interest grid plots stakeholders on two axes: power (their authority to influence the project) and interest (their level of concern about the project's outcomes). This creates four quadrants with different management strategies.

[PAUSE — 3 seconds]

High Power, High Interest — these are your key stakeholders. Manage them closely. Keep them well-informed and actively engaged. Your project sponsor and key executives typically fall here.

High Power, Low Interest — keep these stakeholders satisfied. They have authority but are not closely watching. A senior VP who owns the budget but delegates management falls here. Do not overload them, but never surprise them.

Low Power, High Interest — keep these stakeholders informed. End users and operational teams often fall here. They care deeply but cannot unilaterally change the project. Ignoring them causes resistance during implementation.

Low Power, Low Interest — monitor these stakeholders. Minimum engagement is appropriate. Check in periodically to confirm their position has not changed.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix"]

Beyond identification and analysis, the PM must assess each stakeholder's current engagement level versus their desired engagement level. PMI defines five engagement levels: Unaware, Resistant, Neutral, Supportive, and Leading. The goal is to move each stakeholder toward their desired engagement level — which is usually Supportive or Leading.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

A stakeholder who is Resistant is not just uninformed — they actively oppose the project. Managing a resistant stakeholder requires understanding the source of their resistance: Is it fear of change? Competing priorities? A past bad experience? Communication alone rarely converts a resistant stakeholder. You need to understand their concern and address it specifically.

## Segment 4 — The RACI Matrix (11:30–15:00)

[SHOW SLIDE: "The RACI Matrix — Accountability Made Visual"]

One of the most practical tools in project communication and governance is the RACI matrix. RACI stands for Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. It defines who does what on every task, deliverable, or decision in the project.

[SHOW SLIDE: "RACI Definitions"]

Responsible: The person or people who do the work. They execute the task. There can be multiple R's on one task.

Accountable: The one person who owns the outcome — the person answerable for the task's completion and quality. There must be exactly one A per task. Never zero, never two.

Consulted: People whose input is needed before the work is done. Two-way communication — they provide input and receive feedback on how it was used.

Informed: People who are notified after a decision or action. One-way communication — they receive information but are not asked for input.

[PAUSE — 3 seconds]

The most common RACI error on real projects — and on Project+ exam questions — is assigning two people as Accountable for the same task. When two people are both accountable, each assumes the other is taking ownership, and both ownership and accountability dissolve. One A per task, without exception.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Sample RACI Matrix"]

| Task | PM | Tech Lead | Developer | QA | Sponsor |
|------|----|-----------|-----------|-----|---------|
| Define requirements | A | R | C | C | I |
| Design architecture | I | A | R | C | I |
| Write test plan | I | C | I | A/R | I |
| Approve budget change | I | I | I | I | A |

Notice that the Sponsor is A on budget approval — no one else can authorize that decision. The QA team member is both A and R on the test plan — they own it and they do it. This is allowed when one person is both executing and accountable.

[SHOW SLIDE: "When to Build the RACI"]

Build the RACI matrix during project planning, before execution begins. It is most valuable on cross-functional projects where team members report to different managers. The RACI eliminates the ambiguity of "I thought you were doing that." When every task has a clear owner and the team has agreed to the matrix, task accountability disputes become much rarer.

## Segment 5 — Meeting Facilitation and Status Reports (15:00–19:30)

[SHOW SLIDE: "Running Effective Project Meetings"]

Project meetings are often where project management either happens well or falls apart. A well-facilitated meeting produces decisions and action items. A poorly facilitated meeting produces frustration and more meetings.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

Every project meeting should have four things: an agenda distributed before the meeting, a clear owner or facilitator, a parking lot for off-topic issues, and documented action items with owners and due dates.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Meeting Types in Project Management"]

You need to know these meeting types for the Project+ exam.

Kickoff meeting: Formally launches the project. Introduces team members, reviews the project charter and scope, confirms roles and responsibilities, and establishes communication norms. The kickoff creates shared understanding.

Status meeting: Regular recurring check-in on project progress. Reviews milestones achieved, current issues, risks, and decisions needed. Produces meeting minutes distributed to all stakeholders.

Lessons learned meeting: Conducted at project close (and sometimes at phase gates during the project). Captures what went well, what could be improved, and what should be done differently on future projects.

Change control meeting: Convened when a change request requires evaluation and decision. The Change Control Board reviews the request, its impact on scope, schedule, and cost, and makes an approve/reject/defer decision.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Status Reports — What to Include"]

Status reports are one of the project manager's most important communication tools. A good status report tells stakeholders what they need to know to make decisions — not everything the PM knows.

A standard project status report should include:

- Overall project status (green/yellow/red with brief explanation)
- Milestones completed since last report
- Milestones planned for next period
- Current budget vs. actual spending
- Schedule status (on track, behind, ahead — by how much)
- Open issues and decisions needed
- Top risks and their current status
- Key accomplishments and upcoming activities

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

Status reports should be honest. A project manager who consistently reports green status to avoid uncomfortable conversations is not managing the project — they are managing perceptions. When problems exist, the status report is how the sponsor learns about them in time to help. Surprises at project close are always worse than honest early reporting.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Communication Models — Sender-Receiver"]

The Project+ exam tests the sender-receiver communication model. Communication flows through: sender encodes message → selects channel → message travels → receiver decodes message → receiver provides feedback. Noise — anything that interferes with the signal — exists at every stage.

Key terms: encoding (converting thoughts to message), decoding (interpreting received message), noise (any interference), feedback (receiver's response confirming understanding), and medium (the channel used to transmit the message).

## Segment 6 — Managing Stakeholder Engagement (19:30–22:00)

[SHOW SLIDE: "Managing vs. Monitoring Stakeholder Engagement"]

Managing stakeholder engagement is an active, ongoing process throughout the project lifecycle. It is not sufficient to create a stakeholder register during planning and check it once at project close.

[PAUSE — 2 seconds]

Managing engagement means having regular conversations with key stakeholders, understanding their evolving concerns, addressing issues before they become resistance, and adapting your communication approach based on feedback. Different stakeholders need different approaches — what works for a technically oriented CTO will not work for a frontline end user who just wants to know if their daily workflow is changing.

[SHOW SLIDE: "Common Stakeholder Engagement Strategies"]

Four practical engagement strategies the exam may test:

Active listening: Focus on what the stakeholder is saying — not just what you want to say next. Reflect back what you heard. Ask clarifying questions. Stakeholders who feel heard become allies; stakeholders who feel dismissed become resistors.

Issue log: A formal record of raised concerns, decisions, and resolutions. The issue log creates accountability — stakeholders know their concerns were recorded and will be addressed.

Conflict resolution: Addressing disagreements directly and early is always better than avoiding them. The collaborating style — working to find a solution that satisfies both parties — produces the best long-term outcomes.

Change communication: Stakeholders resist change most when they do not understand it. Communicate the why — the business need driving the change — before the what. "We are implementing a new system because the current one costs $400,000 per year in maintenance fees" lands very differently than "you are getting a new system and retraining."
