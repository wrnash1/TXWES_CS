## Scenario A — The Stakeholder Who Derailed the Launch

A county government IT department launched a new online permitting portal to replace a paper-based process. The project manager conducted stakeholder identification during planning and listed 11 stakeholders including the IT director, two deputy directors, the project sponsor, and the development team. The Building Inspections Division — the largest operational user of the new system with 47 employees — was not included in the stakeholder register because the PM assumed they would simply adapt to whatever system was delivered. The permitting portal went live on schedule and within budget. Three weeks later, the Building Inspections Division Director formally escalated a complaint to the county administrator that the portal lacked a critical field used in all commercial permit applications, making the system unusable for their primary workflow. The county administrator ordered the project reopened and an additional $85,000 scope expansion was funded.

Sub-questions for Scenario A:

1. Identify the specific stakeholder management process failure that caused this outcome. Which step of the stakeholder management process was either skipped or performed incorrectly, and what should the PM have done at that step to prevent the escalation?
2. Using the Power-Interest grid, analyze where the Building Inspections Division Director should have been placed and what engagement strategy that quadrant requires. How does the correct quadrant assignment change the communication approach for this stakeholder compared to what the PM actually did?
3. Design a communication plan entry specifically for the Building Inspections Division for a project of this type. Include the information type, communication method (push/pull/interactive), frequency, format, and a brief description of what two-way communication actions should have occurred during requirements definition.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Scenario B — The RACI That Nobody Followed

A financial services firm is implementing a new compliance reporting platform. During planning, the project manager built a detailed RACI matrix assigning clear roles across 22 tasks. At the first milestone review, three deliverables were incomplete. Investigation revealed the following: the data validation report had two Accountable owners — the compliance analyst and the IT lead — and neither had acted because each assumed the other was handling it. The security review deliverable had no Accountable owner because the PM "forgot to assign one." The vendor integration specification was complete, but it had been reviewed and revised by six people — all of whom were listed as Consulted — without any single person coordinating the changes, resulting in conflicting versions.

Sub-questions for Scenario B:

1. Using the RACI framework, identify and name the specific error in each of the three deliverable situations described. For each error, state the RACI rule that was violated and describe exactly how the matrix should have been corrected.
2. The six-person consulted group for the vendor integration specification created a coordination problem. How should the Consulted role be managed to prevent conflicting input? What communication approach — interactive, push, or pull — is most appropriate for gathering Consulted stakeholder input, and why?
3. After discovering these RACI failures at the first milestone review, what specific corrective actions should the PM take for the remainder of the project? Include both immediate fixes to the existing RACI and process changes to prevent recurrence.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Scenario C — The Sponsor Who Stopped Reading Reports

A regional health system is 10 months into a 15-month EHR implementation. For the past four months, the project manager has been sending weekly eight-page status reports to the project sponsor, a Chief Medical Officer with full patient care responsibilities. The CMO has not responded to a single status report and missed the last two steering committee meetings. The PM recently submitted a change request for a $120,000 scope addition that required sponsor approval. Three weeks passed with no response. The PM assumed silence meant approval and directed the vendor to begin the additional work. Two weeks later, the CMO discovered the $120,000 charge during a budget review and was furious, stating she had never seen or approved the change request and that the PM had been sending reports she could not read in the time available.

Sub-questions for Scenario C:

1. Identify the communication management failures in this scenario. Which aspects of the Communication Management Plan were either absent or wrong for this stakeholder? What specific changes to the communication approach should the PM have made after the first missed meeting?
2. The CMO is a High Power, Low Interest stakeholder based on her available bandwidth. What specific communication format, length, frequency, and interaction style is appropriate for this stakeholder type? How does this differ from the eight-page weekly reports the PM was sending?
3. The PM assumed silence meant approval on a $120,000 change request. Using change control and communication management principles, explain why this assumption was incorrect and what formal process the PM should have followed to obtain a documented decision before authorizing the vendor to begin work.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Professor Nash's Note

The hardest part of stakeholder management is not building the register or drawing the Power-Interest grid. Those are just paperwork. The hard part is having the conversation with a resistant stakeholder who does not want to talk to you, or adapting your communication style to a sponsor who has forty minutes a week for your entire project, or finding the one person who was never put on a list but whose approval you actually need. The tools in this module give you a structure — but applying them well requires judgment, observation, and the willingness to ask the uncomfortable question: "Have I actually identified everyone who matters to this project, and do they each have what they need from me?" Ask that question every month, not just at kickoff.
