## Section 2: Quality Management Process Flow
PMI defines three processes in Quality Management.

Plan Quality Management — identifies the quality standards applicable to the project and determines how to satisfy them. The output is the Quality Management Plan, which defines metrics, standards, and the approach to both QA and QC.

Manage Quality — formerly called Quality Assurance, this is the execution-phase process of auditing quality requirements and results to ensure the project is using the defined quality processes. It is proactive and process-focused.

Control Quality — the Monitoring and Controlling process of inspecting completed deliverables to verify they meet the quality requirements. It is reactive and product-focused. The output includes quality control measurements and verified deliverables.

## Section 3: Quality Assurance vs. Quality Control
 QA vs. QC comparison table

The distinction between Quality Assurance and Quality Control is the most frequently tested quality concept on the Project+ exam.

Quality Assurance (QA) is proactive. It focuses on the processes used to produce the work. QA asks: "Are we following the right process?" A QA activity is a process audit — reviewing whether the team is following coding standards, documentation requirements, or testing protocols. QA tries to prevent defects before they occur.

Quality Control (QC) is reactive. It focuses on the product — the deliverable itself. QC asks: "Does this output meet the standard?" A QC activity is an inspection — running tests, reviewing a document for completeness, comparing output against acceptance criteria. QC finds defects after they are produced.

Here is a memory aid: QA Audits processes to Avoid defects. QC Controls products by Checking for defects.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** A question describing a team reviewing their development process against organizational standards is QA. A question describing a team testing completed software modules for bugs is QC. When the exam says "audit" or "process review," think QA. When the exam says "inspection," "testing," or "defect identification," think QC.

The relationship between QA and QC is that QA findings may trigger QC activities, and QC findings often feed back into QA process improvements.

## Section 4: Cost of Quality
 Cost of Quality breakdown diagram

The Cost of Quality (COQ) framework recognizes that quality-related costs fall into two categories.

Cost of Conformance — money spent to prevent defects and ensure quality:

- Prevention costs: training, process documentation, quality planning
- Appraisal costs: testing, inspections, audits

Cost of Non-Conformance — money spent because quality failed:

- Internal failure costs: rework, scrapped deliverables found before delivery
- External failure costs: warranty claims, customer complaints, recalls, legal liability, reputation damage

The goal of quality management is to invest enough in conformance costs to reduce non-conformance costs dramatically. Poor quality is almost always more expensive than the investment required to prevent it.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** COQ questions ask whether a cost is Prevention, Appraisal, Internal Failure, or External Failure. Training = Prevention. Testing = Appraisal. Rework = Internal Failure. Customer complaint response = External Failure. Memorize these four categories.

## Section 5: Seven Basic Quality Tools
 Seven Basic Quality Tools list

PMI identifies seven basic quality tools. Each has a specific purpose and is tested on the exam.

Cause-and-Effect Diagram (Fishbone or Ishikawa): a visual tool that identifies potential root causes of a quality problem. The problem is the fish's head; the bones represent categories of causes (people, process, equipment, materials, environment, measurement). Used to brainstorm causes before implementing corrective action.

 Fishbone diagram example

Flowchart (Process Map): a diagram showing the sequential steps in a process, including decision points. Used to analyze a process for inefficiencies or failure points.

Checksheet: a simple tally form used in real time to record how often specific defects or events occur during QC activities. The data collected is later analyzed using other tools.

Pareto Chart: a bar chart that ranks defect categories from most to least frequent. Based on the 80/20 principle — roughly 80% of defects come from 20% of causes. The PM uses the Pareto chart to prioritize which issues to address first.

 Pareto chart example

Histogram: a bar chart showing the frequency distribution of data. Used to identify patterns in defect data over time.

Control Chart: a chart with upper and lower control limits that monitors process performance over time. Points outside the control limits signal an out-of-control condition requiring investigation.

Scatter Diagram: a graph plotting two variables against each other to reveal whether a correlation exists. Used to test hypotheses about cause-and-effect relationships.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** If an exam question asks which tool helps prioritize which defects to fix first, the answer is Pareto Chart. If a question asks which tool identifies the root causes of a defect, the answer is Ishikawa (Fishbone) Diagram. If a question asks which tool detects an out-of-control manufacturing process, the answer is Control Chart.
