## Section 2: Lifecycle vs. Process Groups — The Critical Distinction [03:30 – 08:30]

[SHOW SLIDE] Side-by-side table: Project Lifecycle vs. Process Groups

I want to start here because this is the number-one source of confusion for students and a very common exam trap.

A project lifecycle is the sequence of phases a specific project goes through. It is tailored to the type of work. A software project might have phases: Requirements, Design, Development, Testing, Deployment. A hardware installation project might have: Survey, Procurement, Installation, Validation.

Process Groups, by contrast, are a universal management framework that PMI applies to every project, regardless of industry or lifecycle model. The five Process Groups — Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring and Controlling, and Closing — are categories of management activity, not project phases.

Here is the key insight: Process Groups do not map one-to-one to phases. In a multi-phase project, each phase typically has its own Initiating, Planning, and Closing activities. And Monitoring and Controlling does not happen at the end — it runs in parallel with Executing from the moment work begins.

[SHOW SLIDE] Multi-phase project diagram showing Process Groups repeating within each phase

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** If an exam question asks "which comes last — Executing or Monitoring and Controlling?" the answer is that they run in parallel, and Closing is the final Process Group. Many students incorrectly say Monitoring and Controlling is last.

## Section 3: The Five Process Groups in Depth [08:30 – 16:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Five Process Groups — overlapping circles with M&C spanning the middle

Let me walk through each Process Group, its purpose, and its most important outputs.

### Initiating

Purpose: formally authorize the project and identify who is involved.

Key outputs:

- Project Charter — authorizes the project and names the PM
- Stakeholder Register — identifies all stakeholders

The charter is signed by the project sponsor, not the PM. Initiating answers the question: "Do we have permission to do this project?"

### Planning

Purpose: develop the comprehensive roadmap before work begins.

Key outputs:

- Project Management Plan (the master plan integrating all subsidiary plans)
- Scope Baseline (Scope Statement + WBS + WBS Dictionary)
- Schedule Baseline
- Cost Baseline
- Risk Register
- Communications Management Plan
- Procurement Management Plan
- Stakeholder Engagement Plan

Planning is the most document-intensive Process Group. The Project Management Plan is not one document — it is a collection of subsidiary plans, one for each Knowledge Area.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** The Project Management Plan is a Planning output, not an Initiating output. The Project Charter is the Initiating output. Students mix these up constantly.

### Executing

Purpose: carry out the work defined in the plan.

Key activities: directing and managing project work, managing quality, acquiring and developing the team, managing communications, implementing risk responses, conducting procurements.

The PM's role in Executing shifts from planning to coordinating — making sure the team has what it needs, issues are resolved, and vendors are delivering.

### Monitoring and Controlling

Purpose: track performance, identify variances, and take corrective or preventive action.

Key activities: monitoring project performance against baselines, performing integrated change control, validating and controlling scope, controlling the schedule and cost, monitoring risk and communications.

[SHOW SLIDE] M&C running in parallel with Executing diagram

This Process Group is unique because it runs continuously throughout the project, not just at a specific time. While the team is executing work, the PM is simultaneously monitoring and controlling.

The most important output of M&C is the change request. When actual performance deviates from the plan, the PM submits a change request to bring the project back on track or adjust the baseline.

### Closing

Purpose: formally end the project or phase.

Key activities: obtaining final acceptance from the customer, closing contracts with vendors, releasing resources, archiving project documents, and documenting lessons learned.

The Closing Process Group produces the Lessons Learned Register — the organizational knowledge gained from the project.

> **Project+ Exam Trap:** A project is not closed when the last deliverable is finished. It is closed when formal acceptance is obtained, contracts are closed, and administrative closure is complete. Skipping Closing is a common PM mistake that creates legal and financial liability.

## Section 4: Project Lifecycle Models [16:00 – 19:30]

[SHOW SLIDE] Predictive vs. Adaptive lifecycle comparison table

Not all projects use the same lifecycle structure. PMI recognizes three primary lifecycle types.

### Predictive (Waterfall)

All scope is defined upfront. The project moves through phases sequentially: you complete Requirements before Design, Design before Build, and so on. Changes are managed through formal change control.

Best for: well-understood, stable requirements. Examples: network infrastructure upgrades, hardware deployments, regulatory compliance implementations.

### Iterative and Incremental

Work is done in repeated cycles. Each iteration delivers a working increment of the product. Requirements evolve based on feedback from each iteration.

Best for: complex products where some requirements are unclear at the start.

### Adaptive (Agile)

Requirements and solutions evolve through collaboration with the customer. Work is done in short time-boxed sprints, delivering working product at the end of each sprint. We will cover Agile in depth in Module 12.

Best for: software development projects with rapidly changing requirements and high stakeholder involvement.

## Section 5: The Project Charter in Depth [19:30 – 22:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Project Charter components diagram

The Project Charter is the single most tested document in the Initiating Process Group, so let me walk through every component.

A complete Project Charter contains: the project title and description, the business case (why we are doing this), project objectives (SMART), high-level scope (in-scope and out-of-scope), high-level milestones, approved budget summary, known risks, key stakeholders, and the sponsor's signature.

The charter does NOT contain detailed schedules, WBS breakdowns, or detailed risk plans. That level of detail belongs in Planning.

The charter serves three functions: it formally authorizes the project, it names the PM and grants resource authority, and it provides the business justification that all future decisions reference.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** Questions about "what formally authorizes the project" and "what grants the PM authority to use resources" both point to the Project Charter. Memorize this. The Charter is always produced in Initiating, always signed by the Sponsor, and never contains detailed planning artifacts.
