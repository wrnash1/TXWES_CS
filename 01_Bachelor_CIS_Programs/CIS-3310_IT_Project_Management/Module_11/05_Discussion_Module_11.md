## Scenario A — The Risk That Was Seen and Ignored

A mid-sized insurance company launched a 12-month IT infrastructure modernization project with a $1.8 million budget. During the kickoff risk identification session, one team member raised a concern that the network equipment vendor had recently experienced supply chain disruptions and that lead times for critical switches had extended from 4 weeks to 16 weeks. The project manager acknowledged the comment but did not add it to the risk register because, in her words, "I don't want to start this project on a negative note." Eight months into the project, the team attempts to order switching equipment and discovers a 16-week lead time that will delay the project by four months and trigger $220,000 in penalty fees.

Sub-questions for Scenario A:

1. Identify every risk management process failure in this scenario and explain which specific PMI risk management process was violated at each point. What should the project manager have done differently when the supply chain risk was first raised?
2. Had the risk been entered into the register and analyzed using the P-I matrix, what probability and impact scores do you believe it would have received given the information available at kickoff? Calculate a risk score and classify its priority zone. Justify your scores with evidence from the scenario.
3. Recommend a specific risk response strategy that would have been appropriate for this supply chain risk, describe the specific actions the PM should have taken, and explain whether any residual or secondary risks would have resulted from that response.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Scenario B — The Opportunity That Became a Threat

A healthcare technology company is developing a new patient scheduling platform. During a risk review meeting, the team identifies an opportunity: a competing product is rumored to be delayed, which could allow early market entry worth an estimated $500,000 in additional first-year revenue. The PM decides to exploit the opportunity by accelerating the development schedule — compressing a 10-month timeline to 7 months by working the development team 60-hour weeks for the final eight weeks. Three months into the acceleration, two of the four senior developers resign, citing burnout. The schedule is now eight weeks behind the accelerated target, and the team must hire replacement contractors at a significant cost premium.

Sub-questions for Scenario B:

1. The PM selected the Exploit strategy to capture the opportunity. Evaluate whether this was the correct strategy choice and whether it was implemented responsibly. What alternative opportunity response strategies might have achieved a better outcome?
2. The developer resignations and contractor cost premium are new risks that emerged from the PM's response action. Using correct risk management terminology, name these risk types and explain what the PM should have done during response planning to anticipate and prepare for them.
3. Reconstruct the risk management process as it should have been applied to this opportunity from identification through monitoring. Be specific about what the PM should have done at each step before and during implementation of the response.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Scenario C — The Project with No Risk Budget

A regional logistics company is deploying a new warehouse management system (WMS) across six distribution centers. The project has a $900,000 budget. During project planning, the PM develops a thorough risk register with 18 identified risks, qualitative analysis scores, and a recommended contingency reserve of $135,000 (15% of budget) to cover the top six risks. The CFO reviews the project plan and removes the contingency reserve entirely, stating: "We don't budget for failure. If you manage the project right, you won't need it." The PM proceeds without any contingency reserve. Four months later, a data integration issue with the existing ERP system — a risk that was in the register with a medium priority score — materializes and requires $95,000 in unplanned rework.

Sub-questions for Scenario C:

1. Using risk management principles, explain why the CFO's reasoning — "if you manage the project right, you won't need it" — reflects a misunderstanding of what contingency reserves are and what they represent. What is the difference between a contingency reserve and a management reserve, and why does every well-managed project need both?
2. The ERP integration risk was in the register at medium priority. Should it have been elevated to high priority given the context of the project? What additional qualitative factors — urgency, proximity, and strategic impact — should the PM have used to argue for a higher reserve allocation?
3. With no contingency reserve and $95,000 in unexpected costs, describe three specific funding recovery options available to the PM. For each option, explain the risk management or project management implications of pursuing it.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Professor Nash's Note

The single most common risk management failure I see in real IT projects is not a failure to identify risks. Most experienced teams can generate a reasonable risk list. The failure is in the follow-through — the risk gets added to the register on day one and nobody looks at it again until the risk has already materialized. Risk management is not a planning exercise you complete and archive. It is an ongoing monitoring discipline. Risks change priority over time. New risks emerge. Response actions create their own risks. The PM who reviews the risk register at every status meeting, challenges the team to surface new risks, and tracks triggers actively is the PM whose projects survive contact with reality.
