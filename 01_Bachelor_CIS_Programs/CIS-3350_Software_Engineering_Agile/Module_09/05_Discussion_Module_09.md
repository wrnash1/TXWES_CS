## Scenario A — The WIP Limit That Nobody Respects

The Mesa Dev Team recently adopted Kanban after six months of Scrum. They configured a board with a WIP limit of 3 for the Development column. Within two weeks, the column routinely holds 6–8 items. When asked about it, developers say: "The limit is too low. We have eight developers — why can only three things be in development at once?" The team lead adds: "The WIP limit is hurting our throughput." The Scrum Master (who coaches this team alongside a Scrum team) is considering removing the WIP limit entirely.

Sub-questions:

1. What misunderstanding about WIP limits is the team demonstrating? Use Little's Law in your answer to explain why a low WIP limit can actually improve throughput, not reduce it.
2. Why is consistently exceeding the WIP limit more harmful to flow than the limit itself? Reference the concepts of context switching and cycle time in your answer.
3. What should the coach do instead of removing the WIP limit? What steps could help the team understand and respect the limit?

## Scenario B — The Value Stream Nobody Mapped

DesignFirst is a software consultancy where feature requests follow this path: business analyst writes requirements (3 days), requirements sit in a committee queue (7 days), committee reviews and approves (1 day), developer reads requirements and emails 10 clarifying questions (4 days to resolve), developer builds the feature (5 days), feature waits for code review (3 days), code review completed (1 day), feature waits for QA availability (2 days), QA tests and approves (2 days), feature deployed to production (1 day). Total: 29 days. The CTO believes the process is "fast enough" and resists any changes.

Sub-questions:

1. Identify which steps in this process are value-adding, which are non-value-adding but necessary, and which are pure waste. Classify at least six of the ten steps and justify each classification.
2. The 10 days of waiting time (committee queue + code review wait + QA availability wait) represent what Lean waste category? Why is waiting time waste rather than a necessary part of the process?
3. Apply Lean Principle 3 (Create Flow) to this process: identify two specific structural changes that would reduce or eliminate the largest delays, and estimate the cycle time savings each change could produce.

## Scenario C — Scrum or Kanban? The Argument

The TechBridge team is six months old and has been running Scrum. Half the team — led by the backend developer Jordan — wants to switch to Kanban. The other half — led by the Product Owner Alex — wants to stay in Scrum. Jordan argues: "We get interrupted constantly with urgent fixes. Sprint Planning is pointless when half our Sprint gets blown up by production issues. Kanban would give us flexibility." Alex responds: "We have a product roadmap and quarterly stakeholder presentations. We need the structure of Sprints and Sprint Goals to keep us aligned." Neither side is entirely wrong.

Sub-questions:

1. Based on the team's described situation (feature development plus frequent production interruptions), evaluate Jordan's argument for Kanban and Alex's argument for Scrum. Which framework characteristics does each argument correctly cite?
2. What specific Scrumban configuration could resolve this tension? Describe two concrete practices the team could adopt that honor both the need for Sprint structure and the need for flow management of urgent interruptions.
3. What question should the team ask itself to determine how much of their work is planned feature development versus unplanned support and interruptions? How would the answer to that question guide the Scrum vs. Kanban vs. Scrumban decision?

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

Kanban looks simple on the surface — a board with columns and colored cards — but its discipline is in the limits and the data. Teams that skip WIP limits turn their Kanban board into a to-do list with no flow management. Teams that skip metrics turn Lean into a decoration rather than a practice. The most interesting Kanban question for most software teams is not "Scrum or Kanban?" but "how much of our work is predictable enough for Sprint commitments, and how much is so unpredictable that we need pull-based flow?" The answer is almost never 100% one or the other. That is why Scrumban exists — not as a compromise, but as an honest response to the reality of most software teams' work.
