## Instructions

Choose one of the three scenarios below (A, B, or C). Write an initial post responding to all three sub-questions within your chosen scenario. Then respond to at least two classmates who chose different scenarios whenever possible.

Initial post length: 175–225 words. Peer responses: at least 75 words each, adding substance beyond agreement.

## Scenario A — The Overloaded Product Owner

Sarah is the Product Owner for a fintech startup's mobile banking app. She also serves as the Head of Product for the company, which means she sits in three executive meetings per day, writes investor reports weekly, and is pulled into sales calls for key accounts. When her Scrum Team holds Sprint Planning, Sarah is often unavailable. Developers have started making their own priority decisions rather than waiting for her. The Scrum Master, frustrated, has started ordering the Product Backlog herself to "keep things moving."

Sub-questions:

1. What specific Scrum accountability is being violated here, and by whom? Cite the Scrum Guide's language about the Product Owner's accountability.
2. The Scrum Master ordering the Product Backlog seems helpful on the surface. Why is it actually a problem for the team's long-term Scrum health?
3. What two concrete steps could help resolve this situation without reorganizing the entire company? Consider what the Scrum Guide permits regarding delegation.

## Scenario B — The "Helpful" Manager

Marcus is a senior manager at a retail company. His company's Scrum Teams have been given authority over their Sprint Backlogs. Marcus, however, has a habit of walking into team rooms during Sprints and verbally assigning "priority fixes" directly to Developers, bypassing the Product Owner and the Sprint Backlog process entirely. He justifies this by saying: "I need to make sure important things get done — I can't wait until the next Sprint." The Developers comply because Marcus is their line manager.

Sub-questions:

1. Name the specific Scrum principle Marcus is violating. What does the Scrum Guide say about who manages the Sprint Backlog and how changes to Sprint scope should be handled?
2. What role should the Scrum Master play in this situation? What specific actions should the Scrum Master take, and with whom?
3. Marcus's concern — that important things might not get done fast enough if he has to wait — is a legitimate business concern. How does well-executed Scrum actually address this concern through its framework?

## Scenario C — Too Many Meetings

A Scrum Team at a healthcare software company conducts all five Scrum events as defined, but also attends: a Monday morning all-hands status meeting (1 hour), a Wednesday architecture review (2 hours), a Friday project report meeting run by the PMO (90 minutes), and ad-hoc "sync" calls initiated by stakeholders throughout the week. Developers are complaining that they spend more time in meetings than writing software. The Scrum Master responds: "The Scrum events are mandatory — I can't eliminate the others."

Sub-questions:

1. Is the Scrum Master correct that the extra meetings must stay? What does the Scrum Guide actually say about the relationship between Scrum events and other organizational meetings?
2. Agile Manifesto Principle 8 relates to sustainable development. How does this meeting-heavy environment connect to that principle, and what is at risk if it continues?
3. What actions can the Scrum Master take to protect the team's focused work time, and what Scrum value supports these actions?

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

The Scrum framework's elegance is in its simplicity — three accountabilities, five events, three artifacts. But simplicity does not mean easy. The hardest part of Scrum is not learning the framework; it is protecting it when organizational pressures push against it. Every scenario in this discussion represents a real pattern I have seen in professional environments. When you understand why each Scrum rule exists, you can defend it intelligently instead of just citing a rulebook.
