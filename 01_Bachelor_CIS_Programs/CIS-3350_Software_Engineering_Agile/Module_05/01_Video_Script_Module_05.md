## Section 1 — Welcome and Sprint Closing Events
Welcome to Module 5. We have covered the events that open and maintain a Sprint — Sprint Planning and the Daily Scrum. Today we cover the two events that close every Sprint: the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective.

These two events are among the most misunderstood in Scrum. Teams confuse them with each other, skip them when busy, or run them in ways that produce no useful output. Understanding them precisely — both their purpose and the common failure modes — is essential for the PSM I exam and for running a healthy Scrum team.

By the end of this module you will be able to:

- Explain the distinct purpose of the Sprint Review versus the Sprint Retrospective
- Describe who attends each event and why
- Identify the outputs of each event
- Recognize the most common failure modes for both events
- Explain how these two events implement the empirical pillars of inspection and adaptation

Let's start with the Sprint Review.

---

## Section 2 — Sprint Review: What It Is and What It Is Not
The Sprint Review is the fourth event in the Sprint. It occurs at the end of the Sprint. The entire Scrum Team and key stakeholders attend.

The Scrum Guide describes the Sprint Review as a working session where the Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the outcome of the Sprint and determine future adaptations. The Sprint Review is not a demo where the team shows off their work for approval. It is an inspection and adaptation event — the team and stakeholders collaboratively examine what was built, what they learned, and how the Product Backlog should evolve as a result.

Let me unpack what that means in practice.

During a Sprint Review, the Product Owner explains what Product Backlog items have been Done and what has not been Done. The Developers discuss what went well during the Sprint — not just in terms of process, but in terms of the work itself — and what problems were encountered. Then the team demonstrates the Increment to stakeholders, not as a sales pitch but as a basis for conversation.

The most valuable part of the Sprint Review is not the demo — it is the conversation that follows. Stakeholders see working software and react: 'This is close but the filter should work differently.' 'This is great, but I just realized we need X to support the new regulation.' 'We can simplify this feature — I don't think users need all three options.' That feedback directly updates the Product Backlog.

PSM I Exam Tip: The output of the Sprint Review is a revised Product Backlog. Not approval. Not a sign-off. Not a release decision. A revised Product Backlog that reflects what was learned in the review conversation.

The Sprint Review is timeboxed to four hours for a one-month Sprint, with shorter reviews for shorter Sprints.

Now let me explain what the Sprint Review is NOT.

It is not a status meeting. The Scrum Team is not reporting to management. Stakeholders are partners in an inspection conversation, not an audience being presented to.

It is not a gate. There is no 'pass' or 'fail' for the Sprint Review. Stakeholders do not approve or reject the Increment at the Sprint Review. The Product Owner decides whether to release the Increment based on the Definition of Done and the organization's release strategy — not based on Sprint Review approval.

It is not the only opportunity for feedback. Stakeholders should be engaged continuously throughout the product's development. The Sprint Review is a formal inspection point, but it should not be the first time stakeholders see any output.

## Section 3 — Sprint Retrospective: The Team's Improvement Engine
The Sprint Retrospective is the fifth and final event in the Sprint. It occurs after the Sprint Review and before the next Sprint Planning.

The Sprint Retrospective is the Scrum Team's dedicated time to inspect themselves — their working relationships, their processes, their tools, and their Definition of Done. Its purpose is to create a plan of at least one high-priority process improvement to implement in the next Sprint.

Note that the Retrospective is about the team's process — how they work together — not about the product. The Sprint Review is about the product. These two events have completely different focuses, and confusing them is a common PSM I exam trap.

The Scrum Guide says the Retrospective inspects the Sprint regarding: individuals, interactions, processes, tools, and the Definition of Done. Let me give you examples of what kinds of conversations happen in each area.

Individuals: Are team members experiencing blockers they have not raised? Is any team member feeling overloaded or underutilized? Are skills being developed effectively?

Interactions: Is the Daily Scrum creating alignment or just consuming time? Are Developers collaborating effectively on complex problems or working in silos? Are conflicts being surfaced and resolved or ignored?

Processes: Is our Definition of Done too strict in some areas, or not strict enough in others? Is our refinement process producing ready-enough stories for Sprint Planning? Are we using our tools effectively?

Tools: Is our issue tracker creating friction or reducing it? Are our development and testing tools current and effective? Are we spending too much time on manual tasks that could be automated?

Definition of Done: Has our understanding of quality matured since the last Sprint? Should we add or adjust DoD criteria?

PSM I Exam Tip: The Scrum Guide says the most impactful improvements identified in the Retrospective may be added to the Sprint Backlog as a formal item for the next Sprint. This is how the Retrospective creates accountability — by treating process improvements the same way the team treats product work.

The Sprint Retrospective is timeboxed to three hours for a one-month Sprint.

A common Retrospective format is 'Start, Stop, Continue' — what should the team start doing, stop doing, and continue doing? Another popular format uses a sailboat metaphor: wind (what propels us), anchors (what holds us back), rocks (risks ahead), and the destination (our goal). The format matters less than the outcomes: honest reflection and a committed improvement action.

Teams that skip the Retrospective or treat it as optional are violating Agile Manifesto Principle 12 — 'at regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective' — and they are paying the price in accumulated dysfunction and missed improvement opportunities.

## Section 4 — Closing the Sprint: The Increment and the Feedback Loop
Let me close by connecting the Sprint Review and Retrospective to the larger empirical model.

The Sprint Review implements the product feedback loop: build something, show it to stakeholders, learn what to build next, update the plan.

The Sprint Retrospective implements the process feedback loop: run the Sprint, reflect on how we worked, identify one improvement, implement it next Sprint.

Together, these two events ensure that both the product and the team improve every Sprint. Without the Sprint Review, the team builds in a vacuum. Without the Retrospective, dysfunction accumulates unchecked. Both events are essential for sustainable, high-quality delivery.

PSM I Exam Tip: When a question asks what happens at the end of a Sprint, the correct answer involves both the Sprint Review (product inspection with stakeholders) and the Sprint Retrospective (team process inspection). Questions that treat the Sprint as ending with only one event are testing whether you know both must occur.

PSM I Exam Tip: The Sprint Retrospective occurs after the Sprint Review. The order matters: the team first inspects the product (Review), then inspects themselves (Retrospective), then begins the next Sprint (Sprint Planning).
