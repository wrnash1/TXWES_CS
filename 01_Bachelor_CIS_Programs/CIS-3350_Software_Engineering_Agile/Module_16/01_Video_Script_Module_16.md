## Section 2 — The Scrum Framework: Complete Review [02:30–09:00]

"Let me review the Scrum Framework from the exam's perspective.

[SHOW DIAGRAM: Complete Scrum framework — three accountabilities, five events, three artifacts with commitments]

The three Scrum accountabilities: Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developers. Know these precisely. The exam uses the term 'accountability,' not 'role' — a deliberate choice in the 2020 Scrum Guide. Every PSM I question about what a person 'should do' is really asking you to apply the accountability framework.

Product Owner: the single person accountable for the Product Backlog and maximizing the value of the product. The Product Owner is one person, not a committee. They may represent stakeholder interests but they hold final prioritization authority.

Scrum Master: accountable for the Scrum Team's effectiveness. Serves the Product Owner, the Developers, and the organization. The Scrum Master is not a project manager, does not assign tasks, and does not control the team.

Developers: the professionals who do the work of creating the Increment. Self-managing — they decide how to accomplish their work. Cross-functional — they have all the skills needed to create a Done Increment.

The five Scrum events and their purposes:

[SHOW DIAGRAM: Event cascade — Sprint → Sprint Planning → Daily Scrum → Sprint Review → Sprint Retrospective]

Sprint: the container for all other events. Fixed length, one to four weeks. A Sprint cannot be extended or shortened once started — only cancelled, and only by the Product Owner, only when the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete.

Sprint Planning: creates the Sprint Backlog. Addresses three topics: why (Sprint Goal), what (selected PBIs), how (initial plan). The entire Scrum Team participates.

Daily Scrum: 15-minute event for Developers. Inspects progress toward Sprint Goal. Creates a plan for the next 24 hours. The Scrum Master does not run the Daily Scrum — Developers self-organize it.

Sprint Review: inspects the Increment and adapts the Product Backlog. Stakeholders attend. It is a working session, not a presentation or report.

Sprint Retrospective: inspects the Scrum Team's process, interactions, and practices. Creates a plan for improvement. The most actionable improvements often target the Definition of Done.

The three artifacts and their commitments:

Product Backlog — commitment: Product Goal. Sprint Backlog — commitment: Sprint Goal. Increment — commitment: Definition of Done.

PSM I Exam Tip: The commitment structure — the Product Goal, Sprint Goal, and Definition of Done — was added in the 2020 Scrum Guide. Older study materials may not include it. Every artifact has exactly one commitment."

## Section 3 — Commonly Tested Concepts [09:00–15:00]

"Let me walk through the exam topics that generate the most errors on the PSM I.

[SHOW DIAGRAM: Exam hotspot areas — Definition of Done, Sprint cancellation, Product Owner authority, self-management vs. self-organizing]

Definition of Done: the Definition of Done defines what 'done' means for an Increment. It applies to every Increment. If organizational standards exist, the team's DoD must include them as a minimum. A story that does not meet the Definition of Done is not done — it is not included in the Increment and does not contribute to velocity. The DoD is owned by the Scrum Team; the Developers create it when no organizational standard exists.

Sprint Goal: the Sprint Goal is a commitment that belongs to the Sprint Backlog. It is set during Sprint Planning. Developers may negotiate the scope of Sprint Backlog items with the Product Owner to achieve the Sprint Goal — this is the flexibility within the Sprint. The Sprint Goal itself does not change during the Sprint.

Sprint cancellation: a Sprint can only be cancelled by the Product Owner. No one else has this authority. The only condition that justifies cancellation is when the Sprint Goal has become obsolete. Sprint cancellation is rare.

Self-management: the 2020 Scrum Guide replaced 'self-organizing' with 'self-managing.' The distinction is important. Self-organizing meant the team chose its own structure. Self-managing means the team decides who does what, when, and how — within the Sprint, for the work of the Sprint Backlog. This is a stronger statement of team autonomy.

Product Backlog Refinement: refinement is an ongoing activity, not a formal event. The Scrum Guide says no more than 10 percent of Sprint capacity should go toward refinement. It is not a fifth event — it happens continuously during the Sprint.

Scrum Master as servant-leader: the Scrum Guide describes the Scrum Master as serving the Product Owner, Developers, and the organization. Common exam traps describe the Scrum Master as making decisions for the team, managing the schedule, or directing work. All of these are incorrect.

[SHOW DIAGRAM: What Scrum Master does NOT do — assign tasks, own the Sprint Backlog, run Daily Scrum, track individual performance]

PSM I Exam Tip: When an exam question describes a Scrum Master doing something that looks like project management, directing team members, or reporting to senior management about team performance — the answer is almost always that this violates the Scrum Master's accountability."

## Section 4 — Agile Manifesto and Principles [15:00–18:00]

"The PSM I also tests the Agile Manifesto — the four values and twelve principles. You need to know them by content, not just by number.

[SHOW DIAGRAM: Four Agile values — individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working software over comprehensive documentation; customer collaboration over contract negotiation; responding to change over following a plan]

The four values each state a preference, not an absolute. The items on the right are not without value — the items on the left are valued more. This nuance appears on the exam: 'the Agile Manifesto says documentation has no value' is false. It says working software is valued more.

The most frequently tested principles on the PSM I:

Principle 1: Deliver working software frequently, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference for the shorter timescale. This connects to Scrum's Sprint cadence.

Principle 2: Welcome changing requirements, even late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage. This connects to the empirical process — no fixed plan survives contact with reality.

Principle 7: Working software is the primary measure of progress. Not documentation, not Burndown charts, not velocity.

Principle 9: Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility. This justifies TDD, design patterns, CI/CD, and refactoring as legitimate Agile practices — not extras.

Principle 12: At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly. This is the Sprint Retrospective.

PSM I Exam Tip: When an exam question asks which Agile principle justifies a particular practice — TDD, pair programming, CI/CD, design patterns — the answer is almost always Principle 9 (technical excellence) or Principle 12 (regular reflection)."

## Section 5 — Exam Strategy and Closing [18:00–22:00]

"Let me share the exam strategies that matter most.

Read every question for what it is actually asking. Many PSM I questions present a scenario and ask 'what should the Scrum Master do?' or 'what is the most appropriate response?' The answer is always the one most consistent with Scrum values — transparency, inspection, adaptation, courage, commitment, focus, openness, respect — and the Scrum Guide.

Eliminate answers that describe anti-patterns. If an answer describes the Scrum Master assigning work, the Product Owner controlling the Developers, or the team adjusting the Sprint Goal mid-Sprint, eliminate it immediately.

When the question describes a problem a Scrum Team is experiencing, look for the answer that addresses the root cause within Scrum's framework — a Retrospective action, a Definition of Done update, a refinement improvement — rather than an external management escalation.

The Scrum Guide is the only authoritative source for the exam. Everything else — books, blog posts, scaled frameworks — is supplementary. When the Scrum Guide and a blog post disagree, the Scrum Guide is correct for exam purposes.

[SHOW DIAGRAM: Exam preparation checklist — read Scrum Guide twice, take Scrum.org open assessments, practice 80-question timed exam, review any wrong answers against the Scrum Guide]

The Scrum.org open assessments at scrum.org are free and representative of exam difficulty. Take the Scrum Open assessment until you consistently score above 95 percent before scheduling the real exam. Timing matters — 80 questions in 60 minutes is 45 seconds per question. Timed practice is essential.

This is the end of Module 16 and the end of CIS-3350. It has been a genuine privilege. The Scrum Guide, the Agile Manifesto, the technical practices, the metrics, the scaling frameworks — all of it leads to one practical goal: helping software teams deliver real value to real users, predictably and sustainably.

Good luck on your PSM I exam. Go earn it."
