## SEGMENT 2 — BABOK Knowledge Areas Review (2:30–8:00)

[SLIDE: "The Six BABOK Knowledge Areas"]

The BABOK Guide — the Business Analysis Body of Knowledge — organizes business analysis competencies into six knowledge areas. The ECBA exam tests your understanding of all six. Let me walk through each one with emphasis on the concepts most likely to appear on the exam and that we have covered in this course.

### Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring

This knowledge area is about how you plan and manage your BA work. Key topics: selecting the BA approach (predictive vs. adaptive), conducting stakeholder analysis, planning requirements management, and establishing governance.

The most important concept: stakeholder analysis. You must be able to identify stakeholders, assess their influence and interest, and determine the right engagement approach for each. Exam questions often present a stakeholder situation and ask what the BA should do.

### Elicitation and Collaboration

Elicitation is how you gather information from stakeholders. Key techniques: interviews, workshops, observation, document analysis, surveys, prototyping, and focus groups. Each technique has strengths and appropriate contexts.

The most important concept: selecting the right elicitation technique for a given situation. Exam questions often describe a scenario — geographically distributed stakeholders, a subject matter expert who is difficult to schedule, a large group with conflicting views — and ask which technique is most appropriate.

### Requirements Life Cycle Management

This knowledge area covers maintaining, tracing, prioritizing, and approving requirements. Key topics: requirements traceability, change control, requirements approval, and the difference between requirements states (proposed, approved, verified, validated).

The most important concept: the difference between verifying requirements (confirming they are well-formed and internally consistent) and validating requirements (confirming they actually meet the business need).

### Strategy Analysis

Strategy Analysis covers understanding the business need before defining a solution. Key topics: business need identification, current-state assessment, future-state definition, risk assessment, and change strategy.

The most important concept: the BA's role is to understand the problem before jumping to a solution. Exam questions test whether you know that requirements work begins with understanding the business context, not writing requirements.

### Requirements Analysis and Design Definition

This is the core knowledge area — where most technical BA work lives. Key topics: modeling requirements (use cases, process flows, data models, state diagrams), defining requirements attributes, prioritizing requirements, and specifying and modeling the solution design.

Techniques covered in this course that appear in this knowledge area: data modeling and normalization, process modeling, use cases, wireframes and prototyping, and the requirements traceability matrix.

### Solution Evaluation

Solution Evaluation covers assessing whether a deployed solution delivers intended value. Key topics: solution performance measures, post-implementation evaluation, change management, and identifying solution limitations.

The most important concept: evaluation is an ongoing responsibility, not a one-time post-launch event. A BA assesses solution performance continuously and recommends improvements.

## SEGMENT 3 — ECBA Exam Structure and Strategy (8:00–12:00)

[SLIDE: "ECBA Exam — Structure and Requirements"]

Let me give you the practical facts about the ECBA exam.

The ECBA — Entry Certificate in Business Analysis — is IIBA's entry-level certification. It is designed for professionals who are new to BA or transitioning into BA roles.

Eligibility requirements: 21 hours of professional development in business analysis within the past four years, and agreement to IIBA's Code of Ethics. There is no work experience requirement for the ECBA.

Exam format: 50 multiple-choice questions, 60-minute time limit, online proctored.

Score to pass: IIBA does not publish a specific passing percentage. The exam is scored using a scaled scoring model.

Topic distribution: the exam is weighted across the six knowledge areas. Strategy Analysis and Requirements Analysis and Design Definition typically carry the most weight.

[SLIDE: "Exam Strategy — How to Approach ECBA Questions"]

ECBA exam questions are scenario-based. They describe a situation and ask what the BA should do, or they present a concept and ask you to identify the correct definition or application. Here are strategies that work.

Strategy 1: Read the question before the scenario. Many questions have a stem that tells you exactly what they are testing. Read the question first, then read the scenario with that filter.

Strategy 2: Eliminate clearly wrong answers first. ECBA distractors often include answers that are plausible in isolation but wrong for the specific context. Eliminate the two obviously wrong options, then compare the remaining two.

Strategy 3: When in doubt, think BABOK. The ECBA tests BABOK knowledge, not practical experience. Sometimes the "textbook" answer feels different from real-world practice. On the exam, choose the BABOK answer.

Strategy 4: Do not overthink. Most ECBA questions are testing whether you understand a core concept. If you find yourself constructing an elaborate justification for an unlikely answer, you are probably wrong.

Strategy 5: Manage your time. Sixty minutes for 50 questions is 72 seconds per question. Flag questions that require more thought and return to them. Do not let one hard question consume five minutes.

## SEGMENT 5 — Practice Scenarios (16:00–19:30)

[SLIDE: "Practice Scenario Walkthrough"]

Let's work through four practice scenarios in exam format. I will present the scenario, give you a moment to think, then walk through the answer and the reasoning.

Scenario 1: A BA is assigned to a project for a new customer portal. During initial meetings, the project sponsor describes the solution she wants: "We need a mobile app with account management, billing, and a chatbot." What should the BA do first?

Answer: Understand the underlying business need before accepting the proposed solution as a requirement. The sponsor has described a solution, not a problem. The BA's first step is to ask why — what business problem is the mobile app intended to solve? What is the current-state pain? Only then can the BA evaluate whether this solution actually addresses the need.

BABOK connection: Strategy Analysis — understanding business need before defining solution.

Scenario 2: A BA needs requirements input from fifteen subject matter experts across four time zones. Meeting times that work for all fifteen are nearly impossible to schedule. Which elicitation technique is most appropriate?

Answer: Survey or questionnaire. With a large, geographically distributed group and scheduling constraints, a survey allows the BA to gather structured input asynchronously. Follow-up interviews can be conducted with a smaller subset for clarification.

BABOK connection: Elicitation and Collaboration — technique selection by context.

Scenario 3: After requirements are approved, a stakeholder requests a change to a core business rule that affects twelve other requirements. What is the BA's correct first action?

Answer: Assess the impact of the change before approving it. The BA performs an impact analysis — identifying which requirements, design elements, and test cases are affected — and presents the full scope of the change to the appropriate decision-making authority for approval.

BABOK connection: Requirements Life Cycle Management — change control.

Scenario 4: A deployed ERP system is performing correctly according to technical metrics, but three months after go-live the projected 30% reduction in manual data entry has not materialized. Only 15% of eligible transactions are being entered through the new system. What has most likely failed?

Answer: Change management and adoption. Technical performance is fine, but users are not using the system as intended. This is a Desire or Knowledge/Ability gap in ADKAR terms — users are reverting to manual processes. The BA should facilitate an adoption assessment and recommend targeted interventions.

BABOK connection: Solution Evaluation — assessing solution performance, change management.

## SEGMENT 6 — Career Pathways (19:30–22:00)

[SLIDE: "BA Career Pathways — Where Do You Go From Here?"]

Let's talk about your career. You have completed a rigorous introduction to business analysis. The ECBA positions you for entry-level BA roles and signals to employers that you take professional development seriously. But where does this go?

The typical BA career progression looks like this.

Entry-level: Junior BA or BA Associate. You support senior BAs on large projects, conduct elicitation under supervision, and build your documentation and modeling skills. The ECBA is the right credential at this level.

Mid-level: Business Analyst or Systems Analyst. You lead requirements for small to medium projects independently. You own elicitation, analysis, and documentation from start to finish. The CCBA (Certificate of Capability in Business Analysis) is the next IIBA credential at this level — requires 3,750 hours of BA experience.

Senior level: Senior BA, Lead BA, or Product Owner. You mentor junior BAs, lead complex multi-stakeholder projects, and shape BA methodology for your organization. The CBAP (Certified Business Analysis Professional) is the highest IIBA credential — requires 7,500 hours of BA experience.

Beyond individual contributor roles: BA Manager, Practice Lead, Director of Business Analysis, or pivot into Product Management, Program Management, or Solution Architecture.

The common thread: BAs who advance are excellent communicators, trusted advisors to business leadership, and technically credible with their development partners. None of those skills become obsolete.
