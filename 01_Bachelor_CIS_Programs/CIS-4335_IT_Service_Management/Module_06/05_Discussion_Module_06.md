## Scenario A: The Overwhelmed Help Desk

A mid-size insurance company's IT help desk handles 350 tickets per day with a team of 8 agents. Performance data shows: average resolution time of 6.4 hours (target: 3 hours), first-contact resolution of 19% (target: 55%), customer satisfaction score of 2.4 out of 5.0, and 40% of tickets are password resets that require no troubleshooting. The IT manager says: "We know we need to improve, but we do not even know where to start. Everything seems urgent."

Respond to all three of the following prompts:

1. Apply the first three steps of the Continual Improvement Model to this scenario. For each step, be specific about what the IT manager should actually do — not just what the step is named.
2. The IT manager says "everything seems urgent." Which Guiding Principle should guide how the team prioritizes improvement initiatives, and how would it specifically apply to the CIR for this organization?
3. The 40% password-reset volume is an obvious automation candidate. Which step of the seven-step model would identify this as an improvement opportunity, and which Guiding Principle would drive the automation decision?

## Scenario B: The Stalled Improvement Register

A government IT agency has maintained a Continual Improvement Register for three years. The current CIR has 94 items. An audit reveals: 61 items have been in "idea" status for more than one year; 12 items are marked "in progress" but have had no update in six months; 8 items were closed as "completed" but no outcome was documented; and only 13 items have ever resulted in a measurable change. Staff have stopped submitting improvement ideas because "they just go into a black hole."

Respond to all three of the following prompts:

1. Using the seven-step model, identify which steps are being skipped or poorly executed in this organization's improvement process. Be specific about the evidence from the scenario that demonstrates each gap.
2. The staff perception that ideas "go into a black hole" represents a failure of which Guiding Principle? Explain how applying that principle would change the way the CIR is managed.
3. Design a 90-day rescue plan for this CIR using at least three steps of the seven-step model. What would the IT director do in each step during the 90-day window?

## Scenario C: The Successful Improvement That Did Not Stick

A logistics company's IT team implemented an improvement to its incident categorization system nine months ago. The project followed all seven steps of the Continual Improvement Model. At the six-month review (Step 6), the results were excellent: average resolution time dropped from 4.1 hours to 1.9 hours, and first-contact resolution improved from 28% to 61%. The team declared success and moved on to the next improvement initiative.

Three months later, a new round of data shows resolution time has crept back up to 3.2 hours and first-contact resolution has fallen to 44%. Investigation reveals that two experienced agents left and were replaced by new hires who were not trained on the improved process, and a system upgrade two months ago partially reverted some of the tool configurations that supported the new workflow.

Respond to all three of the following prompts:

1. The improvement worked initially but degraded. Which step of the seven-step model was not properly executed, and what should the team have done at that step to prevent this outcome?
2. Which of the four ITIL 4 dimensions best explains the degradation caused by the new hires not being trained on the improved process? Which dimension explains the tool configuration reversion?
3. How should the team now use the seven-step model to recover the improvement? Starting from Step 2, walk through the steps with specific actions appropriate to this situation.

## A Note from Professor Nash

Continual improvement sounds simple in theory — find what is broken, fix it, repeat. In practice it is much harder. Organizations fail at improvement not because they lack good ideas, but because they skip the baseline, set vague targets, implement too much at once, or declare success before the change has actually stabilized. The scenarios in this discussion are drawn from patterns I see repeatedly in real IT organizations. The seven-step model is a discipline, not a checklist. Apply it rigorously.
