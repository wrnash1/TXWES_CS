## Scenario A: The Accepted Risk That Materialized

A logistics company maintained a risk register entry for ransomware attacks. The risk was assessed at Likelihood = 3, Impact = 5, score = 15. The CIO formally accepted the risk, noting that the cost of additional controls (air-gapped backups and endpoint detection and response software) was "not in this year's budget." The acceptance was documented and signed. Six months later, the company suffered a ransomware attack that took systems offline for nine days, costing approximately $3.2 million in recovery costs and lost business.

**Prompt:** Was the CIO's risk acceptance decision a valid ITIL-aligned decision? What are the criteria for legitimate risk acceptance vs. negligent risk acceptance? Does having documented acceptance provide legal or regulatory protection? How should this incident change the organization's approach to risk acceptance going forward? Consider whether the cost of controls vs. the cost of the eventual incident changes your analysis.

## Scenario B: The Third-Party Audit Clause

A mid-size SaaS company has grown rapidly and now serves several enterprise clients. A new contract with a large bank includes a clause requiring the bank to have the right to audit the SaaS provider's security controls at any time with 30 days' notice. The SaaS company's CEO is uncomfortable with this clause — they have no SOC 2 report, their change management process is informal, and they know an audit would reveal gaps. They are considering refusing the clause or negotiating it out.

**Prompt:** Advise the CEO on whether to accept or negotiate the audit clause. What risks does accepting it create? What risks does refusing it create? What is the relationship between customer audit rights and SOC 2 certification? If the company invested in pursuing SOC 2 Type II certification instead of resisting audits, how would that change their risk posture and their competitive position?

## Scenario C: Regulatory Conflict

A multinational IT services firm operates in the European Union (subject to GDPR) and the United States (subject to various state privacy laws and HIPAA for their healthcare clients). The GDPR requires breach notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of discovery. Some U.S. regulations allow 60 days. The company's current breach response procedure says "notify regulators within 30 business days." This satisfies some requirements but violates GDPR.

**Prompt:** How should the company's risk register capture this compliance conflict? Should the company adopt the most stringent standard (GDPR's 72 hours) globally, or maintain different procedures by jurisdiction? What are the operational and risk implications of each approach? How does the ITIL guiding principle of "keep it simple and practical" apply here?
