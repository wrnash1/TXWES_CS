## Scenario A — The Click-Rate Trap

A hospital system has run a phishing simulation program for three years. The security awareness manager reports to the CISO that the click rate has dropped from 31 percent to 6 percent — a result the team is proud of. However, the CISO notices that the organization's real phishing reporting rate is near zero: employees almost never use the one-click report button to flag actual suspicious emails. In the same period, the organization experienced two successful business email compromise incidents, both of which employees received but did not report.

Respond to the following in 175–225 words: What does the gap between the low click rate and the near-zero reporting rate tell you about this program's actual effectiveness? Identify which Kirkpatrick level the click rate represents and which level the reporting gap reveals. What specific program changes would you recommend to address the reporting culture problem? Your response should go beyond "add more training" and propose a concrete intervention.

## Scenario B — Executive Exemption

A retail company's security awareness manager has designed a new phishing simulation campaign. When she presents the campaign plan to the CTO, he tells her that the executive team — the C-suite and all VPs — should be excluded from the simulations because "if an executive clicks a phishing link, we don't want that leaking to the board or the press." He adds that executives are "too busy to deal with simulation emails."

Respond to the following in 175–225 words: What are the security risks of exempting the executive team from phishing simulations? What is the CTO's underlying concern, and is there a way to address that concern without creating an exemption that undermines the program? Your response should address both the technical risk of executive exemption and the cultural message it sends to the broader workforce. Reference the connection between leadership behavior and security culture from the module content.

## Scenario C — Culture vs. Compliance

A large logistics company has had a mandatory annual security awareness training program for seven years. Completion rates are consistently above 95 percent. However, the security team observes the following: employees never voluntarily report suspicious emails, password reuse is rampant (evidenced by credential stuffing indicators in SIEM logs), and when a new badge access policy was introduced requiring escorts for visitors, employees routinely ignore it when it inconveniences them.

The CISO concludes: "We have a compliance culture, not a security culture." She wants to propose a culture transformation initiative to the board but needs a concrete argument for why the current compliance-only approach is insufficient and what a culture-focused alternative looks like.

Respond to the following in 175–225 words: How would you explain to the board the difference between a compliance culture and a security culture, using evidence from this scenario? What are the specific risks that a compliance-only culture creates? Propose one concrete initiative — beyond adding more training — that could begin shifting the culture at this organization.

## A Note from Professor Nash

Scenario A describes a real pattern I have observed in mature phishing simulation programs: organizations celebrate declining click rates while overlooking the equally important reporting rate metric. A workforce that does not click simulated phishing links but also never reports real suspicious emails has learned to pass the test, not to protect the organization. The distinction matters enormously when an attacker sends a carefully crafted spear-phishing email that gets through the filters.

Scenario B captures a tension I have personally navigated: balancing legitimate executive communication concerns against the security and cultural imperative of treating leadership identically to the broader workforce. There is a workable solution here — I encourage you to think creatively about it rather than accepting either extreme.

Scenario C is the scenario I find most strategically interesting, because it describes an organization that has done everything the compliance checklist requires and still has a significant human-element risk. The board conversation about culture transformation is one of the most important conversations a security leader can have, and this discussion gives you practice making that argument.

Engage genuinely with each other's posts. The peer response requirement exists because the most useful learning in this forum comes from encountering a perspective you had not considered.

— Professor Nash
