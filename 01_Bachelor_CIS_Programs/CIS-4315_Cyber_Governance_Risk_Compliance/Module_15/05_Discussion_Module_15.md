## Scenario A — The 72-Hour Clock

A regional hospital network headquartered in Houston, Texas, serves approximately 85,000 patients annually. Approximately 3,200 of those patients are EU citizens who receive telemedicine consultations through a partnership with a European health nonprofit. On a Tuesday morning, the hospital's security operations team confirms that a phishing attack has compromised the credentials of a billing department employee and that the attacker accessed a database containing electronic PHI and payment card information for an estimated 4,100 patients, including 310 EU residents.

The hospital's compliance officer states that HIPAA's 60-day notification window gives the team sufficient time to complete the forensic investigation before notifying anyone. The hospital's legal counsel disagrees, citing an obligation to notify the EU supervisory authority within 72 hours.

Address the following in your response. Explain whether the compliance officer or legal counsel is correct and why. Identify every notification obligation this breach triggers, including the applicable framework, the party to be notified, and the deadline. Describe what governance structure — roles, decision-making authority, and documented procedures — should have been in place before this breach occurred so that notification obligations could be executed without this disagreement.

## Scenario B — Compliance Theater vs. Real Security

A mid-sized financial services firm based in Fort Worth, Texas, has maintained PCI-DSS compliance for seven consecutive years and passes its annual QSA assessment without findings. The firm processes approximately 2 million payment transactions annually. This year, a threat actor successfully exfiltrated cardholder data for 48,000 customers over a six-month period through a SQL injection vulnerability in a web application. A post-breach forensic investigation confirms that the firm was technically PCI-DSS compliant at the time of the breach — all twelve requirements were assessed as met by the QSA.

The firm's board of directors asks the CISO how a fully compliant organization could suffer a breach of this scale. The CISO's draft response to the board states that PCI-DSS compliance failed and that the standard should be held responsible.

Address the following in your response. Evaluate whether the CISO's framing is accurate and appropriate for a board communication. Explain the relationship between regulatory compliance and actual security posture, using this scenario as your evidence. Describe what a mature security governance program should include beyond audit compliance to prevent this type of breach, and explain how you would communicate this distinction to a non-technical board.

## Scenario C — The Unified Compliance Decision

A healthcare technology startup has just won contracts with three major clients: a Medicare Advantage plan (triggering HIPAA), a hospital system that accepts payment cards (triggering PCI-DSS), and a state university in California that enrolls EU exchange students (triggering CCPA and potentially GDPR). The startup's CEO, who has a software development background, proposes building three separate compliance programs — one for each regulatory framework — with different policies, different control sets, and different audit tracks. The startup has 42 employees and a $2.1 million annual operating budget.

The newly hired information security manager disagrees with the CEO's approach and wants to propose a unified compliance framework instead.

Address the following in your response. Make the business case to the CEO for a unified compliance approach in language appropriate for a non-technical executive audience. Identify at least two specific controls that would simultaneously satisfy requirements across at least two of the applicable frameworks, and explain how unified documentation of those controls reduces cost and complexity. Address the concern that a unified approach might result in "watered down" compliance that satisfies no framework fully, and explain why this concern is or is not valid.

## Professor Nash — Closing Note

Module 15 covers material that many of you will encounter immediately upon entering the workforce. Whether you work in healthcare, finance, retail, government, or technology, you will operate inside a web of regulatory obligations that shape what your security program must do, what you must report, and when. The scenarios in this discussion are drawn from patterns I have seen play out in real organizations.

The most important professional habit you can develop is the habit of asking not just "are we compliant?" but "are we secure, accountable, and prepared?" Compliance is the starting line. Your judgment as an information security professional determines how far past that line you take your organization.

I look forward to reading your posts. Engage with your classmates' scenarios even if they differ from your own — the breadth of regulatory frameworks in this module means that every scenario teaches something the others do not.
