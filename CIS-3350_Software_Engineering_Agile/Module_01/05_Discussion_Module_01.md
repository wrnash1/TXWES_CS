## Instructions

## Scenario A — The Frozen Requirement Problem

A mid-size insurance company is building a claims processing portal. The project manager insists on using Waterfall and has scheduled a three-month requirements phase followed by a six-month development phase. Two months into development, the state legislature passes a new regulation requiring all claims portals to support a new data field and reporting format. The project manager tells the team the requirements are locked and the change will be deferred to "version 2."

Sub-questions:

1. What is the core risk the project manager's decision creates, and how does the cost-of-change curve explain it?
2. How would a Scrum team handle this same regulatory change mid-project differently?
3. Are there any circumstances where deferring the change to a future version is actually the right call? Explain your reasoning.

---

## Scenario B — Choosing the Right Model

You are a software engineering consultant hired to advise three different clients. Client 1 is building an air traffic control system with strict FAA certification requirements and fully defined requirements. Client 2 is a startup building a social media platform with no existing user base and high uncertainty about which features will gain traction. Client 3 is a defense contractor building a new weapons targeting system with extreme technical risk around sensor fusion algorithms that have never been implemented at this scale.

Sub-questions:

1. Which SDLC model would you recommend for each client, and what is your primary justification for each?
2. The Scrum Guide grounds Scrum in empirical process control. Which of the three clients most needs empirical control, and why?
3. Is it ever appropriate to use elements of multiple SDLC models on the same project? Describe a hybrid approach and its trade-offs.

---

## Scenario C — The 1968 Software Crisis Today

The 1968 NATO conference described a "software crisis" characterized by projects that were over budget, behind schedule, and full of defects. A 2015 report by the Standish Group (CHAOS Report) found that only 29 percent of software projects were completed on time and on budget, suggesting the crisis has not been fully solved.

Sub-questions:

1. In your view, why does the software crisis persist more than 50 years after the 1968 conference identified it? Identify at least two root causes.
2. How do the Agile Manifesto values address the specific failure modes identified in the software crisis? Be specific about which value addresses which failure mode.
3. What does the Scrum Guide mean when it says Scrum is designed for "complex" problems? How does this definition of complexity relate to the persistence of the software crisis?

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

The tension between predictability and adaptability is one of the oldest debates in software engineering — and it has never been fully resolved. As you discuss these scenarios, push yourself beyond "Agile is better than Waterfall." The most useful engineers understand when each approach is appropriate, and they can articulate that reasoning to a skeptical project manager or executive. That argument — grounded in empiricism, the cost of change, and the nature of complexity — is what Module 1 is training you to make.
