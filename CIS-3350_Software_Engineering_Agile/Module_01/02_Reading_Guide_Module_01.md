### Introduction

Welcome to **Module 01 – Software Engineering Overview and SDLC Models**! This week establishes the foundational vocabulary and mental models you will build on throughout the course. You will survey the major software development life cycle (SDLC) models — Waterfall, Spiral, Iterative, and Agile — and learn how to evaluate which model fits a given project context.

Understanding SDLC models is directly relevant to the PSM I exam: the Scrum Guide situates Scrum as an empirical framework that emerged in reaction to the limitations of plan-driven, sequential processes. Knowing those limitations — and why empiricism matters — is the starting point for every subsequent Scrum topic.

---

### 1. High-Yield Glossary

Review these essential definitions carefully. The certification exam expects you to know these concepts inside and out:

* **Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC):** A structured sequence of phases — requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance — that guides the production of software from initial concept to retirement. Different SDLC models prescribe different orderings, overlaps, and feedback mechanisms between these phases.

* **Waterfall model:** A linear-sequential SDLC model in which each phase (requirements → design → coding → testing → deployment) must be fully completed and signed off before the next begins. Its strength is documentation clarity; its weakness is the high cost of late-discovered requirement errors.

* **Agile model:** An iterative, incremental family of SDLC approaches that deliver working software in short cycles and continuously incorporate stakeholder feedback. Agile prioritizes responding to change over following a fixed plan, as stated in the Agile Manifesto (2001).

* **Iterative phases:** A development approach in which the software is built and refined through repeated cycles (iterations), with each cycle producing a working — though incomplete — version of the product. This contrasts with Waterfall's single pass through each phase.

* **Risk evaluation:** The practice of identifying, assessing, and prioritizing potential threats to a project's schedule, budget, or quality early in the life cycle. The Spiral model explicitly embeds risk evaluation as a driving activity at the start of each spiral loop.

### 2. Certification Exam Tips

* **PSM I Focus:** The Scrum Guide does not prescribe any specific SDLC model, but PSM I scenario questions frequently test whether you understand *why* Scrum's empirical approach (transparency, inspection, adaptation) outperforms plan-driven models when requirements are uncertain. Know this distinction cold.
* **Scenario Trap:** Watch for questions that describe a "big design up front" situation and ask what a Scrum team should do. The Scrum answer is always to inspect and adapt through short Sprints — never to freeze requirements for months before building.
* **Agile Manifesto Principle to Know:** "Responding to change over following a plan" — this is the philosophical break from Waterfall. Questions may present a scenario where a client changes requirements mid-project and ask how Scrum handles it (answer: the Product Backlog is updated; existing Sprint commitments are protected).
* **Study Resource:** Read the opening sections of [The Scrum Guide (2020)](https://scrumguides.org/) — the free, official reference for the PSM I exam — which explains *why* Scrum exists as a response to complexity. Supplement with the [Agile Manifesto](https://agilemanifesto.org/) (12 Principles page) to understand the philosophical foundation tested on the exam.

### Lab & Command Integration

In this week's hands-on lab, you will:

* **Document SDLC model characteristics:** Complete a comparison matrix identifying the phases, feedback loops, stakeholder touchpoints, and risk profiles of Waterfall, Spiral, Iterative, and Agile models.
