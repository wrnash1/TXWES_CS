## Scenario A — The Estimation Shortcut

The Nexus Team has been doing Planning Poker for six months. Recently, the team lead — a senior Developer — proposed a shortcut: "I know this codebase better than anyone. Let me just estimate all the stories myself before Sprint Planning and post the numbers to the backlog. It'll save us 45 minutes per Sprint." Two newer Developers say they prefer it this way because "Planning Poker makes me nervous — I don't want to look like I don't know the codebase." The Scrum Master is considering allowing this.

Sub-questions:

1. What specific benefit of Planning Poker is lost when one person estimates all stories alone? Reference the anchoring bias concept in your answer.
2. What does the fact that two Developers feel nervous about estimating reveal about the team's psychological safety, and how might this affect the quality of estimates and Retrospectives?
3. What should the Scrum Master do? What Scrum principles or Agile values support your recommendation?

## Scenario B — Velocity Under Pressure

The Apex Team has delivered 30–35 story points per Sprint consistently for five Sprints. At a quarterly business review, the Director of Engineering announces: "All development teams need to show 20% velocity improvement by end of Q2 or we will consider staff changes." The Product Owner is alarmed and asks the Scrum Master: "Can we just inflate our estimates going forward so our velocity looks higher?"

Sub-questions:

1. If the team inflates their estimates to show higher velocity, what specific downstream effects will this produce on Sprint Planning accuracy, Product Backlog ordering, and stakeholder trust?
2. What is the root misunderstanding behind the Director's use of velocity as a performance target? What should velocity actually be used for?
3. As the Scrum Master, how would you respond to both the Product Owner and the Director? What data or arguments would you use to reframe the conversation?

## Scenario C — The Estimation Debate

During a refinement session, a user story about integrating a third-party payment processor is being estimated. Jordan (frontend) says 5 points. Alex (backend) says 21 points. The difference triggers a 25-minute argument about API complexity, PCI compliance requirements, and third-party documentation quality. The Product Owner, frustrated, says: "Just pick 13 and move on — we're wasting time."

Sub-questions:

1. The wide divergence between Jordan's 5-point and Alex's 21-point estimate is actually valuable information. What does it reveal, and why would simply averaging to 13 be a poor outcome?
2. How should the Scrum Master facilitate this situation? What is the appropriate next step when a Planning Poker discussion reveals fundamental disagreement about a story's scope or technical complexity?
3. The 25-minute discussion exceeded a reasonable timeframe. What can the team change about their estimation process to prevent this in the future, while still capturing the value of divergent estimates?

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

Estimation is one of the most misunderstood practices in Agile. Teams use it wrong (as a commitment), managers misinterpret it (as a productivity metric), and individuals get defensive about their numbers (because they do not understand its probabilistic nature). The scenarios in this discussion surface all three failure modes. When you can explain velocity, story points, and Planning Poker to a skeptical manager in plain language — connecting the practice to its underlying purpose — you are ready for the Scrum Master role.
