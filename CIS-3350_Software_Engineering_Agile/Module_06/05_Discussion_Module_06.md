## Scenario A — The Frozen Backlog

TechFlow's Product Owner, Diana, created a detailed Product Backlog of 85 items at the start of the project. She ordered all 85 items, wrote acceptance criteria for each one, and told the team: "This is our roadmap — we will work through these in order." Six months later, a competitor releases a new feature that changes market expectations significantly. Several of Diana's top-priority items are now irrelevant, and three important new features are not on the backlog at all. Diana is reluctant to change the backlog because "we planned this carefully and I don't want to disrupt the team's focus."

Sub-questions:

1. Which characteristic of the Product Backlog — as defined in the Scrum Guide — does Diana's approach violate? What specific consequences has her approach created?
2. How does Diana's approach reflect a Waterfall mindset rather than an Agile one? Reference at least one Agile Manifesto value in your answer.
3. What steps should Diana take now, and what practice should she adopt going forward to prevent this situation from recurring? Reference the Product Backlog Refinement concept in your answer.

## Scenario B — The Ordering Dispute

The Scrum Team at CloudBuild is having a conflict. The VP of Sales wants the customer reporting dashboard built first (PBI-07, 13 pts) because it would help close a key enterprise deal. The VP of Engineering wants the API security overhaul done first (PBI-03, 21 pts) because the current security posture is a compliance risk. The Scrum Master wants to start with the easiest item (PBI-12, 2 pts) to "build team momentum." The Product Owner, Jamie, has not made a decision yet because they don't want to upset anyone.

Sub-questions:

1. Who has the authority to make the final ordering decision, and what does the Scrum Guide say about the consequences of failing to exercise this authority?
2. Analyze each of the three ordering arguments (Sales, Engineering, Scrum Master) using ordering factors from the Reading Guide. Which argument is strongest from a Product Owner perspective, and why?
3. What process should Jamie follow to gather input, make a decision, and communicate it in a way that maintains trust with all three stakeholders?

## Scenario C — Refinement Neglect

The Horizon Team does not hold regular refinement sessions. At every Sprint Planning, the Product Owner presents the top items from the backlog and the Developers say they need more information before they can estimate or commit. Sprint Planning regularly runs over its timebox. The team ends up committing to items they do not fully understand, which leads to mid-Sprint scope discovery and incomplete Increments at Sprint end.

Sub-questions:

1. What is the relationship between Product Backlog Refinement and Sprint Planning effectiveness? How does the Horizon Team's situation illustrate this relationship?
2. The Scrum Guide says refinement consumes no more than 10% of Developers' capacity. The team's Scrum Master argues: "We don't have time for refinement — we need to spend all our time building." Respond to this argument with specific evidence from Scrum principles.
3. Design a refinement schedule for the Horizon Team. How often should they refine, who should attend, and what should they accomplish in each session? Explain your design choices.

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

The Product Backlog is the team's most important planning tool and the Product Owner's primary accountability. I have seen projects fail not because the team couldn't build the software, but because the backlog was poorly managed — frozen when it should have evolved, unordered when it needed clear priorities, or full of under-specified items that wasted Sprint Planning time. A well-maintained Product Backlog is the difference between a team that is always confused about what to build next and a team that moves with clarity and purpose.
