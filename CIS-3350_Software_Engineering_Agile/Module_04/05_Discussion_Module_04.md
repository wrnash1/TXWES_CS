## Scenario A — The Sprint Goal That Wasn't

The Beta Team has been running Scrum for four months. Their Sprint Planning sessions produce a list of user stories for each Sprint, but the team has never explicitly defined a Sprint Goal. The Scrum Master says: "We don't need a Sprint Goal — the user stories tell us what we're doing." Midway through Sprint 6, the Product Owner receives urgent feedback from a key stakeholder that one feature is entirely unnecessary and a different feature is critical for an upcoming trade show. Without a Sprint Goal, the team has no principled basis for deciding what to do with this information.

Sub-questions:

1. What specific problem does the absence of a Sprint Goal create in this scenario? Reference the Sprint Goal's role as defined in the Scrum Guide.
2. How would a well-written Sprint Goal have helped the team navigate the mid-Sprint stakeholder feedback? Be specific about how Sprint Goal flexibility works.
3. Write a Sprint Goal for Sprint 6 that could unify the work described above (assume the Sprint includes features related to product catalog and trade show scheduling functionality).

## Scenario B — The 45-Minute Daily Scrum

The Gamma Team's Daily Scrum consistently runs 40–50 minutes. The Scrum Master says this is fine because "we're solving real problems and the team is engaged." The Developers have started showing up late because they know it will run long anyway. Two Developers have asked to be excused from Daily Scrum because they "get more done when they're at their desk." The team's Sprint burndown chart shows that impediments are frequently discovered on Day 8 or 9 of a 10-day Sprint — too late to course-correct meaningfully.

Sub-questions:

1. Identify three specific problems created by this Daily Scrum format. For each problem, explain the Scrum principle or empirical pillar it undermines.
2. The Scrum Master defends the long meetings by saying the team is engaged and solving problems. What is wrong with this reasoning from a Scrum perspective?
3. Propose a concrete restructuring of Gamma Team's Daily Scrum that would fit within 15 minutes and still result in effective inspection and adaptation.

## Scenario C — Velocity Pressure

The Delta Team consistently delivers 28–32 story points per Sprint. Their new Director of Engineering has reviewed the metrics and told the Product Owner: "I need this team hitting 45 points per Sprint by next quarter — we have a lot of features to ship." The Product Owner passes this directive to the team at Sprint Planning. The Scrum Master is unsure whether to push back or comply.

Sub-questions:

1. What does the Scrum Guide say about who determines how much work the team takes into a Sprint? What should the Scrum Master's response to this situation be?
2. What are the likely consequences if the team commits to 45 points when their sustained capacity is 28–32? Consider both short-term Sprint outcomes and long-term team health.
3. The Director's underlying concern — more features shipped per quarter — is legitimate. What Scrum-aligned approach could help address that concern without forcing the team to overcommit? Consider the Product Backlog, team practices, and impediment removal.

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

Sprint Planning and the Daily Scrum are where Scrum theory meets daily team reality. They are also where I see the most drift in real organizations — teams that "do Scrum" but skip the Sprint Goal, or run Daily Scrums as status meetings. The failure modes in these scenarios are not hypothetical; every one of them is a pattern I have observed in professional environments. When you can diagnose these failures and articulate the Scrum principle they violate, you are ready for both the PSM I exam and the real world.
