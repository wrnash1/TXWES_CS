## Section 2: Resource Management Process Flow [03:00 – 06:30]

PMI defines six processes in Resource Management.

Plan Resource Management creates the Resource Management Plan — the guide for how physical and team resources will be acquired, managed, and controlled.

Estimate Activity Resources determines the type, quantity, and characteristics of resources needed for each activity.

Acquire Resources obtains the team members, equipment, and materials needed for the project.

Develop Team builds team competencies, interaction, and the overall environment to improve project performance.

Manage Team tracks performance, provides feedback, resolves issues, and manages changes to optimize project performance.

Control Resources monitors the planned versus actual use of physical resources and takes corrective action as needed.

## Section 3: Organizational Structures and PM Authority [06:30 – 10:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Three organizational structures: Functional, Matrix, Projectized

The structure of the organization determines how much authority the project manager has.

In a Functional Organization, employees report to functional managers (IT Manager, Marketing Director). The PM has little to no formal authority and acts as a coordinator. Team members have divided loyalties.

In a Projectized Organization, employees are assigned to projects and report directly to the PM. The PM has full authority. When the project ends, team members move to new projects or leave.

In a Matrix Organization, employees report to both functional managers and project managers. There are three subtypes: Weak Matrix (PM has limited authority), Balanced Matrix (shared authority), and Strong Matrix (PM has more authority than functional manager).

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** Questions about PM authority almost always involve organizational structure. "The PM coordinates but does not direct resources" describes a Functional or Weak Matrix. "The PM fully controls the team" describes a Projectized or Strong Matrix.

## Section 4: RACI Matrix [10:00 – 13:30]

[SHOW SLIDE] RACI matrix example

The RACI matrix is a Responsibility Assignment Matrix that maps each project task or deliverable to the team members responsible for it.

R = Responsible: the person or people who do the work. Multiple Rs are allowed.

A = Accountable: the single person who owns the outcome and is ultimately answerable for the task. Only ONE person can be Accountable per task. This is the key rule.

C = Consulted: people whose input is needed before the work or decision is finalized. Two-way communication. The responsible person asks; the consulted person responds.

I = Informed: people who need to be kept up to date on progress or decisions. One-way communication. They receive information but are not asked to act.

The RACI matrix is one of the most frequently tested tools in both resource management and communications management on the Project+ exam.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** The most common RACI exam question tests the "only one A per task" rule. If a question describes a task where both the PM and a vendor are Accountable, that is a RACI violation. Only one person can own the outcome.

## Section 5: Tuckman's Team Development Model [13:30 – 18:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Tuckman's five stages with characteristics and PM actions

Bruce Tuckman identified five stages of team development. Every project team moves through these stages, and the PM's leadership approach must adapt at each stage.

Forming: Team members meet, are polite, and establish initial relationships. Roles are unclear. The PM should be directive — set clear expectations and structure.

Storming: Team members begin to assert their identities, challenge each other, and compete for influence. Conflict emerges. This is normal. The PM should coach and facilitate conflict resolution. Teams that skip or rush past Storming often regress later.

Norming: The team establishes norms, resolves conflicts, and begins to collaborate effectively. Trust develops. The PM should shift toward a supportive, participatory style.

Performing: The team functions as a high-performance unit. Members are motivated, competent, and focused. The PM can delegate and focus on removing obstacles.

Adjourning: The project ends. Team members are released to other assignments or leave the organization. The PM should recognize contributions and manage the emotional dimension of team dissolution.

> **Project+ Exam Trap:** Students sometimes think Storming means the team is failing. It does not. Storming is a necessary developmental stage. The PM's job is not to prevent it but to navigate the team through it productively.

## Section 6: Conflict Resolution Techniques [18:00 – 21:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Conflict resolution techniques ranked by PMI preference

PMI recognizes five conflict resolution techniques, listed here from most preferred to least preferred.

Collaborate/Problem-Solve: both parties work together to find a solution that fully satisfies both. Win-win outcome. This is always the PMI-preferred approach because it produces sustainable resolutions.

Compromise/Reconcile: both parties give up something to reach a mutually acceptable middle ground. Win-some/lose-some. Used when collaboration is not feasible and a timely resolution is needed.

Smooth/Accommodate: emphasize areas of agreement; downplay the disagreement. Temporary relief. Does not resolve the underlying issue.

Withdraw/Avoid: postpone dealing with the conflict. Sometimes appropriate when the issue is minor or more information is needed, but it allows the conflict to grow if overused.

Force/Direct: one party imposes their position on the other. Win-lose. Generates resentment. PMI identifies this as the least preferred technique.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** "What is the BEST approach to conflict resolution?" — the answer is always Collaborate/Problem-Solve. "What is the WORST approach?" — the answer is Force/Direct. These appear frequently in scenario questions.
