Risk management is one of the most heavily tested knowledge areas on both the CompTIA Project+ exam and the PMI exams, and for good reason. Every project faces uncertainty — things that might happen, might not happen, or might happen differently than planned. The project manager's job is not to eliminate risk; it is to identify, analyze, and respond to risk systematically so that the team is never completely surprised.

## Section 2: Risk Management Concepts and Process Flow [03:30 – 08:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Risk management process chain

PMI defines six processes in Risk Management.

Plan Risk Management creates the Risk Management Plan, which documents how risk activities will be conducted throughout the project — the methodology, roles, timing, thresholds, and risk categories.

Identify Risks determines which risks might affect the project and documents their characteristics in the Risk Register. This is an iterative process — new risks are identified throughout the project, not just at the start.

Perform Qualitative Risk Analysis prioritizes risks by assessing the probability and impact of each identified risk. This produces a risk score that guides which risks receive the most attention.

Perform Quantitative Risk Analysis provides a numerical analysis of the combined effect of prioritized risks on project objectives. Common techniques include Monte Carlo simulation and decision tree analysis. Note: quantitative analysis is not always performed — it depends on project complexity and the value of the investment.

Plan Risk Responses develops options and actions to address prioritized risks, both threats and opportunities.

Implement Risk Responses is the Executing process where approved risk responses are carried out.

Monitor Risks tracks identified risks, monitors residual and secondary risks, evaluates response plan effectiveness, and identifies new risks.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** Know which process group each risk process belongs to. Plan Risk Management, Identify Risks, Qualitative Analysis, Quantitative Analysis, and Plan Risk Responses are all Planning processes. Implement Risk Responses is Executing. Monitor Risks is Monitoring and Controlling.

## Section 3: Probability, Impact, and Risk Scoring [08:00 – 13:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Probability × Impact matrix with risk scoring

Risk analysis relies on two core attributes: probability and impact.

Probability is the likelihood that a risk event will occur. Expressed as a percentage or ordinal scale (low, medium, high).

Impact is the effect on project objectives if the risk occurs. Typically assessed against cost, schedule, scope, and quality.

Risk Score is calculated by multiplying probability times impact. A risk with 60% probability and a High impact receives a higher score than a risk with 90% probability and a Low impact, depending on the impact scale. Risk scores drive prioritization — high-score risks get responses; low-score risks may be watched or accepted.

The Probability and Impact matrix is a standard tool for visualizing risk scores. Risks in the upper right corner (high probability, high impact) are the highest priority. Risks in the lower left corner receive lower priority. The matrix typically uses color coding: red for high-priority risks, yellow for medium, and green for low.

An important concept: PMI defines risk as any uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on project objectives. Risk is not only bad things — it includes opportunities that the team could exploit to enhance project outcomes.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** Students sometimes assume all risks should be eliminated. PMI teaches that risk is a spectrum — some risks are threats (negative impacts) and some are opportunities (positive impacts). The goal is systematic management of both, not elimination of uncertainty.

## Section 4: Risk Response Strategies [13:00 – 19:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Threat response strategies vs. Opportunity response strategies side-by-side

PMI defines five response strategies for threats and four for opportunities. These are the most exam-tested concepts in risk management.

Threat response strategies:

Avoid: Change the project plan to eliminate the threat entirely. This eliminates the risk itself — not just the impact. Example: choosing a different technology to avoid a known compatibility issue.

Transfer: Shift the financial impact of a risk to a third party. This does not eliminate the risk — it assigns the financial consequences to another party. Examples: insurance, fixed-price contracts, performance bonds.

Mitigate: Reduce the probability or impact of a threat to an acceptable threshold. Mitigation does not eliminate the risk but lowers the risk score. Example: conducting additional testing to reduce the likelihood of a defect reaching production.

Accept: Acknowledge the risk and plan no proactive response. Active acceptance involves setting aside contingency reserve. Passive acceptance requires no action until the risk occurs.

Escalate: Transfer the risk response responsibility to a higher authority outside the project — typically the program manager or sponsor — because the risk exceeds the project's authority or resources.

Opportunity response strategies:

Exploit: Take action to ensure the opportunity definitely occurs. The opposite of Avoid.

Enhance: Increase the probability or positive impact of the opportunity. The opposite of Mitigate.

Share: Assign the opportunity to a third party best positioned to capture it. The opposite of Transfer.

Accept: Acknowledge the opportunity but take no active steps to pursue it.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** The most common exam error is mixing up threat and opportunity strategies. Remember the pairings: Avoid ↔ Exploit; Mitigate ↔ Enhance; Transfer ↔ Share. Accept is used for both threats and opportunities. Escalate applies to threats that exceed project authority. Also know the difference between residual risk and secondary risk: a residual risk is the remaining risk after a response is implemented — the leftover risk not fully eliminated. A secondary risk is a new risk created by the response itself. Example: implementing automated failover (mitigate) creates a new risk of failover system failure (secondary risk).

## Section 5: The Risk Register [19:00 – 22:30]

[SHOW SLIDE] Risk Register sample with all required fields

The Risk Register is the primary output of Identify Risks and the central repository for all risk management activities. It is updated throughout the project as risks are analyzed, responded to, and monitored.

Key fields in a Risk Register include:

Risk ID — a unique identifier for tracking

Risk description — a clear statement of the risk event and its cause/effect relationship

Category — the type of risk (technical, external, organizational, project management)

Probability — assessed likelihood of occurrence

Impact — assessed effect on project objectives

Risk score — probability × impact

Risk owner — the team member responsible for monitoring and responding to the risk

Risk response — the chosen strategy and specific action

Response status — planned, in progress, or closed

Residual risk — remaining risk after the response is implemented

Trigger conditions — the early warning signs that a risk is about to occur

The Risk Register is a living document. Risks are added as new ones are identified, scores are updated as conditions change, and risks are closed when they no longer apply.
