## Section 2: Stakeholder Management Process Flow [03:00 – 07:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Four stakeholder management processes

PMI defines four processes in Stakeholder Management.

Identify Stakeholders is an Initiating process. It determines all individuals, groups, and organizations that have an interest in or are affected by the project. The output is the Stakeholder Register. This happens early — during initiation — and is updated throughout the project as new stakeholders are discovered.

Plan Stakeholder Engagement is a Planning process. The team analyzes current engagement levels, defines desired engagement levels, and develops strategies to close the gap. The output is the Stakeholder Engagement Plan.

Manage Stakeholder Engagement is an Executing process. The PM implements engagement strategies — communicating, building relationships, addressing concerns, and managing expectations to keep stakeholders aligned and supportive.

Monitor Stakeholder Engagement is a Monitoring and Controlling process. The PM evaluates whether engagement strategies are working, identifies shifts in stakeholder interests or concerns, and updates the plan as needed.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** Identify Stakeholders is an Initiating process — one of the first things done on any project. Students sometimes place it in Planning. Know the process group for each stakeholder process.

## Section 3: Stakeholder Identification and the Stakeholder Register [07:00 – 11:30]

[SHOW SLIDE] Stakeholder Register sample with key fields

The Stakeholder Register is the primary output of Identify Stakeholders. It documents all known stakeholders and their key characteristics. Fields typically include:

Name and title of the stakeholder or group. Contact information. Role on the project. Stakeholder's interest — what they care about and how the project affects them. Stakeholder's level of influence or authority. Current engagement level (Unaware, Resistant, Neutral, Supportive, or Leading). Desired engagement level (where the PM wants the stakeholder to be). Communication preferences and constraints.

Identifying stakeholders requires looking beyond the obvious project team. Consider: sponsors and executive leadership; functional managers; end users and customer representatives; regulatory agencies and oversight bodies; vendors and contractors; project-affected communities; and competing projects that might vie for the same resources.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** The Stakeholder Register is NOT the Communications Management Plan. The register documents who the stakeholders are and their characteristics. The Communications Management Plan documents what information they need, when, in what format, and through what channel. Both are required; they serve different purposes.

## Section 4: Stakeholder Analysis — Power/Interest Grid [11:30 – 16:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Power/Interest Grid with four quadrants labeled

The Power/Interest Grid is a stakeholder analysis tool that maps stakeholders based on their level of authority (power) and their level of concern about project outcomes (interest). The four quadrants produce four management strategies.

High Power, High Interest: Manage Closely. These stakeholders have significant authority over project decisions and are deeply invested in outcomes. They should be engaged frequently, involved in key decisions, and kept informed of all significant developments. Examples: the project sponsor, senior executives who championed the project, key customer representatives.

High Power, Low Interest: Keep Satisfied. These stakeholders have significant authority but are not closely following day-to-day project activities. They should receive periodic high-level updates and be engaged immediately if significant changes arise. Examples: C-suite executives with budget authority who are not operationally involved.

Low Power, High Interest: Keep Informed. These stakeholders are closely watching the project and may be significantly affected by it but have limited authority to influence decisions. They should receive regular updates and have a clear channel to raise concerns. Examples: end users, front-line staff affected by the change.

Low Power, Low Interest: Monitor. These stakeholders have minimal influence and low investment in outcomes. They should be monitored for changes in interest or influence but require minimal active engagement. Examples: peripheral departments minimally affected by the change.

## Section 5: Stakeholder Engagement Levels and the Engagement Plan [16:00 – 20:30]

[SHOW SLIDE] Stakeholder engagement continuum

PMI defines five stakeholder engagement levels along a continuum.

Unaware: The stakeholder does not know about the project or its potential impact on them.

Resistant: The stakeholder is aware of the project but opposed to it and its outcomes.

Neutral: The stakeholder is aware of the project but neither supportive nor resistant.

Supportive: The stakeholder is aware of the project and supportive of it and its outcomes.

Leading: The stakeholder is actively engaged and promoting the project's success.

The Stakeholder Engagement Plan documents the current engagement level (C) and the desired engagement level (D) for each stakeholder. The PM's strategies are designed to move each stakeholder from C to D. For example, a key user group currently at Resistant needs a strategy different from one that is currently Neutral and needs to be moved to Supportive.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** The engagement assessment matrix plots C (current) and D (desired) for each stakeholder. When C and D are the same, no change is needed. When D is higher than C, the PM must develop an engagement or communication strategy to move the stakeholder. Exam questions sometimes describe a gap between current and desired engagement and ask which action is appropriate.
