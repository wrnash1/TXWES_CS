## Scenario 1: The Inherited Project

Marcus has just been assigned as project manager for a software implementation project
that is three months into a six-month timeline. The previous PM left the organization
abruptly. Marcus discovers the following conditions: there is no project charter on
file, the scope statement is outdated and has not been updated since month one, three
change requests are sitting in a shared folder marked "pending" with no action taken,
the team has not had a status meeting in six weeks, and the vendor contract has a
milestone payment due in two weeks for a deliverable that has not been accepted.

### Initial Post Prompt

Marcus is overwhelmed but knows he needs to stabilize the project before it can be
managed effectively. Using your knowledge from across this entire course, identify the
five most urgent actions Marcus should take in his first week, in priority order.
For each action, name the project management process area it belongs to and explain
why it is urgent. Your response should be 200–225 words.

### Model Response (200–225 words)

Marcus's five most urgent actions, in priority order:

First — Locate or reconstruct the project charter (Integration Management; Initiating).
Without formal authorization documentation, Marcus has no confirmed authority. If no
charter exists, he must obtain one from the sponsor before taking any binding actions.

Second — Review and adjudicate the three pending change requests (Integrated Change
Control; Monitoring and Controlling). Unresolved change requests create ambiguity about
what is currently in scope. Marcus must convene the CCB immediately and either approve,
defer, or reject each request, then update the project plan accordingly.

Third — Assess the vendor deliverable against contract acceptance criteria (Procurement
Management; Monitoring and Controlling). A milestone payment is due in two weeks. Marcus
must determine whether the deliverable actually meets acceptance criteria before authorizing
payment. Paying without acceptance eliminates contractual leverage.

Fourth — Update the scope statement and communicate current baseline to the team
(Scope Management; Planning). The team cannot execute reliably against an outdated
scope. Even a brief written scope summary circulated to stakeholders restores alignment.

Fifth — Hold a team status meeting and assess schedule and cost performance using EVM
data (Schedule and Cost Management; Monitoring and Controlling). Six weeks without
communication means Marcus has no idea whether the project is on track. He cannot
manage what he cannot measure.

### Peer Response Prompt

Does your classmate's priority order match yours? If you would reprioritize any of
their five actions, explain your reasoning using a specific project constraint or risk.

## Scenario 2: The Exam Question Dilemma

Priya is studying for the Project+ exam and encounters the following practice question:

"A project manager identifies that a key vendor is consistently delivering work two
days late. The project has ten weeks remaining. The PM has attempted to discuss the
issue with the vendor's project coordinator, but nothing has changed. What should the
PM do next?"

A. Issue a stop-work order immediately
B. Escalate to the vendor's executive sponsor and document the conversation in writing
C. Invoke the liquidated damages clause in the contract
D. Add schedule buffer to the remaining work packages

Priya is torn between B and C. She argues that since the contract has a liquidated
damages clause, invoking it (option C) is the most contractually direct response.
Her study partner argues that B is correct because escalation should come before
invoking remedies.

### Initial Post Prompt

Who is right, and why? Apply the exam strategy principles from Module 16 to explain
how to reason through this type of question. Then identify what additional information
in the scenario would change your answer toward C. Your response should be 200–225 words.

### Model Response (200–225 words)

Priya's study partner is correct — B is the right answer — and the reasoning process
illustrates a core Project+ exam strategy: follow the process, and exhaust all
collaborative remedies before invoking contractual penalties.

Liquidated damages are a contractual enforcement mechanism, not a communication tool.
Invoking them immediately — without first escalating to executive leadership and
documenting the outcome — is both premature and likely to damage the vendor relationship
without producing faster delivery. Option B follows the process: escalate with written
documentation, which creates an auditable record and gives the vendor a formal notice
that performance is unacceptable.

The exam strategy principle at work here is: "when in doubt between a collaborative
remedy and a punitive remedy, choose collaboration first unless the scenario explicitly
signals that collaboration has already failed." The scenario states that the PM
discussed the issue with the vendor's project coordinator — not their executive sponsor.
Escalation has not been fully exhausted.

The answer shifts toward C if the scenario adds: the PM has already escalated to the
vendor's executive sponsor in writing, received no corrective action, and the delays
are now causing measurable critical path impacts. At that point, invoking liquidated
damages is the appropriate contractual escalation — all softer remedies are exhausted.

This is how Project+ questions are designed: context changes the answer.

### Peer Response Prompt

Your classmate worked through a similar question using a different principle from the
exam strategy section. Does their principle apply here as cleanly as yours? Is there
a scenario where your two principles would point to different answers?

## Scenario 3: Reflecting on the Course

This is the final discussion of CIS-3310. You have completed sixteen modules, a series
of labs and quizzes, and built a body of knowledge that covers the full Project+
certification domain.

Consider the following: Research consistently shows that people who articulate what
they have learned, connect it to prior experience, and identify specific future
applications retain and transfer knowledge far more effectively than those who simply
complete coursework and move on.

### Initial Post Prompt

Write a reflection that addresses three things. First, identify the single concept,
tool, or framework from this course that surprised you most — something that changed
how you think about IT projects or technology work generally. Second, describe a
specific situation from your professional or academic experience where you could have
applied something you learned in this course. Be concrete. Third, identify one
Project+ domain where you feel genuinely strong and one where you know you still need
work before your exam. Your response should be 200–225 words.

### Model Response (200–225 words)

The concept that surprised me most was Earned Value Management. I expected project
performance measurement to feel abstract and theoretical, but once I worked through
the EVM formulas in the lab, I realized how powerful a tool it is for communicating
project health to a non-technical audience. A CPI of 0.84 tells a sponsor more, faster,
than a paragraph of narrative status update. The precision and brevity of EVM metrics
are genuinely elegant.

A situation where I wish I had this knowledge: last year I was part of a student team
building a capstone application. We lost track of where we were in the project because
we had no formal scope baseline and no performance measurement process. We ended up
cutting three features in the final week because we had not been tracking our progress
against the plan. A simple burndown chart and weekly EVM check-in would have surfaced
the problem six weeks earlier.

My strongest domain is Project Planning — the WBS, scheduling, and EVM concepts clicked
early and I feel confident applying them in both exam questions and real scenarios.
My weakest domain is Project Closing, specifically the formal steps of administrative
and procurement closure. The sequence of closure activities does not feel as intuitive
to me as the planning tools, and I plan to review the closing checklists in the reading
guide twice more before my exam date.

### Peer Response Prompt

Find a classmate whose "surprised by" concept is different from yours. Write a response
that connects their concept to yours — how do the two tools or frameworks interact or
reinforce each other in a real project context?

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

This is my final note to you in CIS-3310.

Scenario 1 is the situation most new project managers face in their first real PM role.
You rarely inherit a perfect project. You often inherit a project in trouble, with
documentation gaps, open issues, and pressure from every direction. What separates
the project managers who succeed from those who do not is not whether they face chaos —
it is whether they know the process well enough to stabilize it quickly.

Scenario 2 captures something I want you to carry into the exam room: the answer on
Project+ is almost always the one that follows the right process in the right sequence.
Trust the process, even when a more aggressive option feels satisfying.

Scenario 3 is for you. Reflection is not a soft skill — it is how professionals
convert experience into expertise. The project managers I respect most are the ones
who can articulate not just what they did, but what they learned and how they would
do it differently. That habit of reflection is the difference between someone with
ten years of experience and someone with one year of experience repeated ten times.

It has been an honor to teach this course. Go pass that exam.
