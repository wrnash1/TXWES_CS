## Overview

Choose one of the three scenarios below (A, B, or C). Write an initial post responding to all three sub-questions for your chosen scenario. Then reply to at least two classmates who chose different scenarios from yours.

## Scenario A: The Feature That Would Not Stop Growing

A software development team at a financial services firm is building a client portal that allows customers to view account balances, download statements, and message their advisors. The original Scope Statement was signed by the VP of Client Services seven months ago. Since then, the team has quietly added push notifications, a budgeting calculator, and a document vault — all in response to informal Slack messages from product managers saying "that would be cool." The project is now six weeks late and $55,000 over budget.

Sub-questions for Scenario A:

1. Using precise PMI terminology, identify what happened. Distinguish whether this is scope creep, gold plating, or both — and explain the difference.
2. What specific scope management documents should have prevented this situation, and what specifically would each document have done to stop the unauthorized additions?
3. The VP of Client Services now loves the added features and wants them to stay. As the PM, how do you handle this? What process must the features go through, and what does that process produce?

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Scenario B: The Missing Piece

A university IT department builds a WBS for their new campus identity management system. Level 2 categories are: Project Management, Requirements, System Design, Development, Testing, and Deployment. A senior auditor reviewing the WBS at the end of the project asks: "Where is end-user training? Where is the go-live support plan? Where is the system documentation for the operations team?" The PM replies: "Those are obvious — everyone knows they're included." The auditor disagrees.

Sub-questions for Scenario B:

1. Which fundamental WBS rule did the PM violate? Explain what that rule requires and why the PM's "it's obvious" argument is insufficient from a project management standpoint.
2. What are the practical consequences of omitting end-user training, go-live support, and system documentation from the WBS? Be specific — trace the impact through at least two other project management knowledge areas.
3. How should the PM have built the WBS to prevent this gap? Describe the process of creating a WBS and the verification step that catches missing deliverables.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Scenario C: The Acceptance Argument

A city government IT department completes a new permitting system and delivers it to the Planning Department (the customer). The Planning Department director says: "This isn't what we wanted — the search function doesn't work the way we described, and the reporting module is missing two data fields we specified." The IT PM responds: "Those were not in the written scope — your team never submitted those requests through our change control process." The Planning Department director is furious and escalates to the city manager.

Sub-questions for Scenario C:

1. What scope management documents should the IT PM reference to support the position that the disputed features were not in scope? How do those documents resolve the dispute?
2. The Planning Department claims the missing features were "always implied." How does the Exclusions section of the Scope Statement protect the PM in this situation, and what lesson does this illustrate for every IT project?
3. When the PM presents the system for formal acceptance, which PMI process is being performed? What should have happened at earlier milestones to prevent this dispute from surfacing only at final delivery?

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

Scope management is where projects are won or lost before a single line of code is written. Every hour you invest in writing precise scope statements and building complete WBS structures pays back ten-fold in avoided rework, avoided conflict, and avoided late nights. As you respond this week, think about the last time you were on a team — school project, work project, volunteer effort — where the scope was unclear. What did it cost you? What would a well-defined scope have changed?
