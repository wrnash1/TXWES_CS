## Scenario A: The Contract That Left the Buyer Exposed

A city government hired a software vendor to build a custom permitting system. Due to time pressure from elected officials, the project manager skipped the make-or-buy analysis and moved directly to negotiating a contract. The scope of work was not finalized when the contract was signed — the SOW referenced only high-level functional objectives. The PM selected a Cost Plus Fixed Fee contract at the sponsor's request, reasoning that "flexibility is more important than cost control." Fourteen months into the 18-month project, actual costs have consumed 95% of the budget with only 60% of functionality delivered. The vendor claims all work aligns with the SOW because the SOW was too vague to define otherwise.

Sub-questions for Scenario A:

1. Identify every procurement management error in this scenario. For each error, explain what the PM should have done instead and which PMI procurement process or principle was violated.
2. Why is a Cost Plus Fixed Fee contract particularly risky when the Statement of Work is vague? What contract type would have better protected the buyer in this situation, and what precondition would have been required before using it?
3. The city is now in a contract dispute. Using PMI's Control Procurements process, describe three specific actions the PM should take to manage this dispute and protect the project's remaining budget and timeline.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Scenario B: The Lowest Bid Problem

A regional hospital is procuring a healthcare analytics platform through a formal competitive bidding process. The source selection criteria were defined as: 30% technical capability, 25% healthcare implementation experience, 25% data security certifications, and 20% price. Three vendors responded. Vendor A scored highest on technical capability and security (weighted score: 82). Vendor B had moderate scores across all criteria (weighted score: 74). Vendor C had the lowest technical and security scores but submitted a price 35% below the other vendors (weighted score: 61). The hospital's CFO reviews the results and overrides the PM's recommendation to award to Vendor A, directing the team to award to Vendor C because "we need to stay within budget."

Sub-questions for Scenario B:

1. Was the source selection process conducted correctly up to the point of the CFO's override? Evaluate the criteria, weighting, and scoring process against PMI's guidance on source selection.
2. The CFO's override changes the award from the highest-scoring vendor to the lowest. What is wrong with this approach from a procurement management perspective? What risks does this decision create for the project?
3. How should the PM respond to the CFO's directive? Describe a professional and PMI-aligned approach that respects the CFO's authority while protecting the project's interests.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Scenario C: The Vendor Who Went Out of Business

A telecommunications company signed a Fixed-Price Lump Sum contract with a cybersecurity firm to conduct a network penetration testing engagement and deliver a final report within 90 days. The contract price was $85,000. Six weeks into the engagement, the cybersecurity firm announces it is ceasing operations due to financial insolvency. The vendor has completed approximately 40% of the work and submitted one partial deliverable. The PM must now decide how to recover the engagement.

Sub-questions for Scenario C:

1. Under the Fixed-Price contract, what financial protections does the buyer have, and what is the buyer's realistic recovery position given that the vendor is insolvent? Explain using contract type risk distribution principles.
2. The PM must procure a replacement vendor to complete the remaining 60% of the engagement. Should she use the same contract type (Fixed-Price Lump Sum) for the replacement engagement, or should she consider a different contract type? Justify your recommendation considering the scope, timeline, and risk factors.
3. What provisions in the original contract, if they had been included, could have mitigated the financial impact of vendor insolvency? Identify at least two specific contract provisions or procurement risk management actions.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

Procurement mistakes are expensive in ways that other project management mistakes are not, because they involve legal agreements. A poorly scoped SOW, a contract type mismatched to the work, or a source selection process that gets overridden at the last minute — each of these can cost an organization far more than the original contract value. The PM's job in procurement is to slow down the process enough to get it right: complete the make-or-buy analysis, define the scope before signing, match the contract type to the level of uncertainty, set selection criteria before reading proposals, and close contracts formally. Every shortcut in procurement eventually shows up as a dispute, a cost overrun, or a vendor relationship that did not survive first contact with reality.
