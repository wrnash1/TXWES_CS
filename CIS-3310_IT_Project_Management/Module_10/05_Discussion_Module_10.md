## Scenario A — The Hidden Schedule Crisis

A state government IT project to replace a legacy benefits administration system has a total budget of $4,000,000 and a 24-month schedule. At the month 12 status review, the project manager reports that the project is "on budget" because the team has spent exactly $2,000,000 — precisely 50% of the total budget at the exact halfway point. The project sponsor is satisfied and approves continuation without any corrective action. However, a newly hired project analyst calculates the following EVM data: `PV = $2,000,000`, `EV = $1,600,000`, `AC = $2,000,000`. Using these figures, `CPI = 0.80` and `SPI = 0.80`.

Sub-questions for Scenario A:

1. The project manager claimed the project is "on budget." Is this claim accurate based on EVM data? Explain precisely what the CPI of 0.80 and SPI of 0.80 indicate about the true project status, and calculate the EAC using the CPI-trend formula to show what the project is actually forecasting.
2. Why is comparing actual spending to the time-phased budget (spending $2M at month 12 of a $4M project) an unreliable indicator of project health? What specific piece of information does this comparison omit that EVM captures?
3. If you were the project analyst presenting these findings to the sponsor, what three actions would you recommend to address the identified performance problems? Connect each recommendation to a specific EVM metric or finding.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Scenario B — The Recovering Project

An enterprise cloud migration project began the year with a BAC of $750,000. By the end of month 4, the project had the following metrics: `PV = $300,000`, `EV = $240,000`, `AC = $280,000`, giving a CPI of 0.857 and an SPI of 0.80. The project manager immediately implemented corrective actions: reassigned two underperforming contractors, replaced a problematic third-party integration tool, and restructured the remaining work packages. By the end of month 8, the updated metrics show: `PV = $600,000`, `EV = $570,000`, `AC = $610,000`, giving a CPI of 0.934 and an SPI of 0.95.

Sub-questions for Scenario B:

1. Compare the month 4 and month 8 metrics. What specific improvements occurred in both cost and schedule performance? Calculate the EAC at month 8 using the CPI-trend formula and compare it to the EAC at month 4 to quantify the impact of the corrective actions.
2. CPI research suggests that once CPI stabilizes, it rarely improves significantly. Yet this project improved CPI from 0.857 to 0.934. What factors in the scenario might explain this improvement, and under what conditions might you trust the month 8 CPI trend as sustainable?
3. At month 8 the project still has a negative CV and a CPI below 1.0. Should the sponsor consider the project recovered? Construct the argument both for and against declaring recovery, using specific EVM values.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Scenario C — The Project That Looks Fine Until It Doesn't

A cybersecurity infrastructure upgrade project has a BAC of $600,000 and a 9-month timeline. For the first six months, the project consistently reported SPI values between 0.97 and 1.02 and CPI values between 0.96 and 1.03 — all close to 1.0 and apparently healthy. The project manager presented these numbers to leadership each month as evidence of strong performance. At the start of month 7, the team discovers that 15% of all completed integration work must be redone due to a standards compliance error that was present in the original design but undetected in quality reviews. This rework will cost $95,000 and add six weeks to the schedule.

Sub-questions for Scenario C:

1. Recalculate the project's EVM metrics after incorporating the rework discovery. Assume the following month 7 data before rework: `EV = $400,000`, `AC = $390,000`, `PV = $420,000`. After rework costs are added: `AC becomes $485,000`. Recalculate CV, CPI, EAC (CPI method), and VAC, and explain what changed.
2. The EVM metrics for months 1–6 showed near-perfect performance, yet a significant defect existed throughout. What does this reveal about the limitations of EVM as a project monitoring tool? What complementary monitoring practices could have detected the compliance error earlier?
3. After the rework discovery, the project manager must present a revised forecast to leadership. How should she frame the communication — what should she say, what data should she present, and how does she maintain credibility when previous reports indicated the project was performing well?

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions in complete sentences.

## Professor Nash's Note

EVM numbers do not manage projects — project managers do. The real skill in EVM is not the arithmetic. Anyone can divide EV by AC. The real skill is knowing what to do with the answer. When CPI is 0.80, you do not just report 0.80 and move on. You dig into why: Is it a scope creep problem? An estimation problem? A vendor performance problem? A rework cycle? The formula tells you that something is wrong. Your job as a PM is to figure out what is wrong and take specific, traceable corrective action before the problem compounds across the remaining work. Practice connecting every number to a decision.
