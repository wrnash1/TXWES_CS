## Section 2: The Communications Management Process Flow [03:00 – 07:00]

PMI defines three processes in Communications Management.

Plan Communications Management creates the Communications Management Plan, which documents who needs what information, when they need it, in what format, and through which channel. This is done during Planning and is updated as stakeholder needs evolve.

Manage Communications is the Executing process. This is where you actually create, collect, distribute, store, and retrieve project information. Status reports go out, meeting minutes are distributed, and updates are sent to stakeholders based on the plan.

Monitor Communications is a Monitoring and Controlling process. You evaluate whether the communications plan is working — are stakeholders getting the information they need? Are they satisfied with the frequency and format? If communications are not effective, you update the plan and improve the approach.

[SHOW SLIDE] Communication management process chain

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** Know which process group each communications process belongs to. Plan Communications Management is in Planning. Manage Communications is in Executing. Monitor Communications is in Monitoring and Controlling. Exam questions often ask which process is the correct context for a described activity.

## Section 3: The Communication Channels Formula [07:00 – 11:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Communication channels formula with worked example

The communication channels formula is one of the most frequently tested calculations on the Project+ exam. The formula is:

Number of channels = n times (n minus 1) divided by 2

Where n is the number of people involved.

Let me walk you through the logic. Each person on a project potentially communicates with every other person. If you have three people — A, B, and C — the communication pairs are A-B, A-C, and B-C. That is three channels. The formula confirms this: 3 times 2 divided by 2 equals 3. Correct.

Now consider a more realistic scenario. You have a project with 10 stakeholders. Channels equal 10 times 9 divided by 2, which equals 45 channels. If three new stakeholders are added, bringing the total to 13, the new channel count is 13 times 12 divided by 2, which equals 78 channels. Adding only three people increased communication complexity by 33 channels. This is why managing stakeholder communication becomes exponentially harder as teams grow.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** The two most common errors on communication channels questions are forgetting to divide by 2 (students get n times n minus 1 and stop), and misidentifying n. Read the question carefully — n includes everyone who communicates, including the PM, sponsor, and key vendors. A common question type asks: "A project had 5 stakeholders and 3 new stakeholders joined. How many new communication channels were added?" You must calculate channels before and after, then subtract: 5 stakeholders = 10 channels; 8 stakeholders = 28 channels; new channels added = 28 minus 10 = 18.

## Section 4: Communication Methods — Push, Pull, and Interactive [11:00 – 16:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Push, Pull, Interactive communication methods with examples

PMI categorizes communication methods into three types. Understanding which type applies in a given scenario is an important exam skill.

Interactive communication involves two or more parties exchanging information in real time. Both parties send and receive simultaneously. Examples include meetings, phone calls, video conferences, and instant messaging. Interactive communication is best used when immediate feedback is needed, the message is complex, or when misunderstandings need to be avoided.

Push communication sends information to specific recipients who need it, without expecting an immediate response. The sender pushes the information out. Examples include status reports, emails, memos, letters, and faxes. Push communication is appropriate for distributing updates to stakeholders who need to be kept informed but do not need to respond in real time.

Pull communication makes information available for recipients to retrieve on their own schedule. Examples include project websites, shared document repositories, knowledge bases, and intranets. Pull communication is appropriate for large audiences, optional reading, or reference materials that stakeholders access when needed.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** Match the communication method to the scenario. If the exam describes a situation requiring immediate, back-and-forth exchange — that is Interactive. If the PM is distributing a weekly report — that is Push. If the PM posts project documents to a shared drive for team members to access — that is Pull. The scenario almost always contains a cue.

## Section 5: The Communications Management Plan and Barriers [16:00 – 21:00]

[SHOW SLIDE] Communications Management Plan components

The Communications Management Plan documents the communication approach for the entire project. It is a component of the Project Management Plan and is developed during Plan Communications Management. Key components include:

Who needs information: a stakeholder directory matched to communication needs

What information they need: project status, risk updates, deliverable approvals, budget reports

When they need it: daily, weekly, monthly, or milestone-driven

How it will be delivered: format (report, presentation, dashboard) and channel (email, meeting, SharePoint)

Who is responsible for sending it: the PM, team leads, or subject matter experts

How to escalate issues: the escalation path if a communication is not acknowledged or a problem arises

The Communications Management Plan should also address communication constraints — for example, a board member who is not available by email and requires a weekly printed summary.

[SHOW SLIDE] Communication barriers and noise model

Effective communication requires a clear sender, an accurate message, an appropriate medium, and a ready receiver. Barriers that degrade communication quality include language and terminology differences, physical distance and time zones, cultural differences in directness and communication norms, information overload, technology failures, and noise in the channel. A good Communications Management Plan anticipates these barriers and builds mitigations.

> **Project+ Exam Tip:** A formal, written communication is required for contractual matters and scope changes. If the exam describes a sponsor requesting a scope addition verbally, the PM should respond by initiating a formal written change request — not by verbally agreeing to proceed. Knowing when to use formal written vs. informal communication is tested frequently.
