## Scenario A: The Disappearing Float

A state agency is implementing a new benefits processing system. The project has a critical path of 42 days running through activities: Requirements (5) → Architecture Design (8) → Development (15) → Integration Testing (7) → UAT (5) → Deployment (2). A parallel path through Requirements → Data Migration (12) → Integration Testing also feeds into Integration Testing. The data migration team used all 6 days of float on the data migration activity due to unexpected data quality issues, and now the project manager discovers that integration testing is at risk of starting late.

Sub-questions for Scenario A:

1. Explain what happened to the float and why the entire path to Integration Testing is now critical. Use the concept of shared float in your answer.
2. The project manager considers two options: crash Integration Testing by adding a second tester (adds $4,000 and reduces duration from 7 to 5 days), or fast-track by beginning UAT planning while Integration Testing is still running. What are the specific risks and costs of each option? Which would you recommend?
3. At the end of the project, the PM documents that the data quality issues caused the float loss. Where should this finding be documented, and how can future projects at the agency benefit from it?

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Scenario B: The Optimistic Estimate

A cybersecurity firm is deploying a new Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) platform for a client. The lead architect, who will personally perform the system configuration activity, estimates it will take 3 days. Other team members privately believe it will take closer to 10 days based on past SIEM deployments. The project manager is suspicious of the 3-day estimate but includes it in the schedule without further analysis. Two weeks into the project, the architect reports the configuration will take 9 more days — seven days longer than planned. This single delay pushes the project deadline past the contractual due date, triggering a financial penalty.

Sub-questions for Scenario B:

1. What estimating technique should the project manager have used for the configuration activity, and what would the result have been? Show the calculation using the optimistic estimate of 3 days, a most-likely estimate of 8 days, and a pessimistic estimate of 13 days.
2. How does schedule float relate to this situation? If the configuration activity had 5 days of total float built in, would the penalty have been triggered? Explain your reasoning.
3. What communication practice should the PM have employed when she suspected the estimate was too optimistic? Reference a specific project management concept in your answer.

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Scenario C: The Parallel Paths Problem

A telecommunications company is upgrading its network operations center with new monitoring software. The project manager creates an initial schedule with all activities sequenced end-to-end (Finish-to-Start), producing a critical path of 60 days. The project director says the deadline is 45 days. The PM analyzes the schedule and identifies three pairs of activities that could potentially be fast-tracked and two critical path activities that could be crashed with additional budget.

Sub-questions for Scenario C:

1. Explain the difference between fast-tracking and crashing in this context. For each technique, describe one specific risk or cost the PM must evaluate before recommending it to the project director.
2. The PM fast-tracks two pairs of activities, recovering 8 days, and crashes one activity by adding a resource, recovering 5 more days — now at 47 days. She needs 2 more days of compression. What factors should guide her decision about whether to crash another activity or fast-track another pair?
3. After all compression is applied, the critical path is now 45 days, but three different paths through the network each have only 1–2 days of float. How does this near-critical-path situation change the PM's monitoring approach for the rest of the project?

Your initial post should be 175–225 words and address all three sub-questions.

## Professor Nash's Closing Note

Schedule management is where project management gets mathematical, and I know that makes some of you nervous. But here is the thing — the math is simple arithmetic. The skill being tested is not algebra; it is reasoning. Can you look at a network diagram and understand why one path matters more than another? Can you evaluate two compression options and explain which one fits the situation? That analytical thinking is what separates a project manager from a task tracker. This week's discussion is your chance to practice that reasoning out loud.
