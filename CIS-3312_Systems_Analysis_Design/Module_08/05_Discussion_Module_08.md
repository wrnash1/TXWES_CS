## Scenario A: The Incomplete Cost Model

A BA at a regional healthcare network presents a feasibility study for a new patient scheduling system to the CFO. The study shows an ROI of 60%, a payback period of 2.5 years, and a positive NPV of $340,000. The CFO reviews the analysis and asks: "Did you include ongoing maintenance costs? What about annual licensing renewals? What about the productivity loss during the 6-month staff training and transition period?" The BA realizes she only modeled development costs and projected annual benefits — she did not account for any post-deployment costs.

Sub-questions:

1. Explain what Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) means and why the BA's analysis is incomplete. Identify at least three categories of ongoing operating costs that a complete cost model should include.
2. Explain how the omission of post-deployment costs would affect each of the three financial metrics the BA reported: ROI, payback period, and NPV. Would each metric increase, decrease, or stay the same if operating costs were added correctly?
3. The BA argues that the CFO should approve the project anyway because the intangible benefits — improved patient experience and reduced scheduling errors — are significant. Describe how a BA should professionally document intangible benefits and explain why they should not be used to compensate for an incomplete financial model.

## Scenario B: The Feasibility Conflict

A university is evaluating a proposal to replace its legacy student information system with a modern cloud-based platform. The IT director says the new platform uses well-established technology and his team has implemented similar systems before — he rates technical feasibility as high. The finance team projects strong ROI. However, the registrar's office reports that faculty and staff are already overwhelmed with recent changes and that morale is low. The legal counsel raises a concern that the cloud provider's data processing agreement may not fully comply with FERPA (the federal student privacy law). Two dimensions pass easily; two are at risk.

Sub-questions:

1. Identify the two feasibility dimensions that are at risk and explain specifically what evidence from the scenario supports each concern.
2. For each of the two at-risk dimensions, propose one concrete mitigation action the project team could take to address the concern before a go/no-go decision is made.
3. The project sponsor argues that since two dimensions pass and the financial case is strong, the project should proceed immediately. Evaluate this argument. Is it valid to proceed when one or more feasibility dimensions are unresolved? Explain the professional standard a BA should apply in this situation.

## Scenario C: Positive NPV, Rejected Project

A BA completes a cost-benefit analysis for a proposed customer loyalty rewards platform. The NPV over 5 years is positive ($420,000). ROI is 45%. The payback period is 3.2 years. Despite these strong numbers, the executive team votes not to proceed. The CEO explains: "The projected benefits assume 70% customer adoption within 12 months. Our last two technology rollouts achieved 28% and 31% adoption in year one. The financial model is technically correct, but the assumptions behind it are not credible."

Sub-questions:

1. Explain what the CEO is identifying about the limitations of cost-benefit analysis. What is the term for the phenomenon the CEO is describing (systematic overestimation of benefits), and why does it pose a risk to investment decisions?
2. Describe at least two techniques a BA can use during cost-benefit analysis to test whether the benefit assumptions are realistic and defensible.
3. The BA is asked to revise the analysis using the organization's actual historical adoption rate (30%) rather than the 70% assumption. Explain how changing the benefit assumption would affect the NPV result, and describe what the revised analysis would need to show for the project to pass economic feasibility.

## A Note from Professor Nash

A financial model is only as good as its assumptions. Students often focus intensely on getting the ROI formula right — and they should — but the more important professional skill is questioning whether the numbers you put into the formula are credible. A technically correct calculation built on optimistic benefit projections and incomplete cost data produces a misleading result that can lead organizations to invest millions in projects that should never have been approved. The BA's job is not just to calculate; it is to challenge, verify, and present an honest picture of the investment case to the people making the decision.
