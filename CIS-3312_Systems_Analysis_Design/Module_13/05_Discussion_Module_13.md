## Scenario A — The Stakeholder Who Wants to Skip Wireframes

A large municipal government is building a new citizen services portal that will allow residents to pay utility bills, apply for permits, and report service issues. The project has a fixed budget and a twelve-month timeline. The CIO tells the BA assigned to the project: "We don't have time for wireframes. Let's just start building and I'll give feedback when I see the real screens."

The BA knows from experience that skipping wireframes typically leads to costly rework in later development stages. However, the CIO is a senior stakeholder with strong influence over the project.

For your initial post, address the following questions. What specific risks does skipping wireframes create in this project context? How would you make the business case to the CIO for at least a lightweight wireframing phase? What is the minimum viable wireframing approach you could propose that respects the timeline constraint while still providing meaningful design validation? How does the citizen-facing nature of this portal strengthen the case for wireframing?

## Scenario B — Throwaway Prototype That Won't Die

A healthcare software company built a throwaway prototype of a patient intake form to demonstrate a concept to a potential client. The prototype was built in four days by two developers using rapid tooling, with no input validation, no security controls, and hardcoded sample data. The demonstration was a success.

Three weeks later, the BA discovers that the development team has been told by a manager to "just finish the prototype — the client wants to go live with it in sixty days." The developers are uncomfortable but feel pressured to comply. The BA realizes that a throwaway prototype is about to become a production system.

For your initial post, address the following questions. What technical and business risks does deploying a throwaway prototype as a production system create? What is the BA's professional responsibility in this situation, and what actions should the BA take? How could this situation have been prevented during the original prototyping phase? What documentation or agreement should have been in place from the start?

## Scenario C — High Fidelity Too Early

A startup is building a mobile expense-tracking application for small businesses. The founder hired a UI designer who spent three weeks building a high-fidelity interactive prototype with custom animations, branded color palettes, and pixel-perfect layouts. The prototype looks impressive.

During the first stakeholder review session, a major investor spends the entire meeting commenting on the color choices and animation speed. Core workflow questions — whether the expense categorization model is correct, whether the approval routing logic makes sense, whether the mobile-first design serves field employees — are never discussed.

When the BA tries to redirect the conversation to workflow questions, the investor says: "The design is basically done. Let's move to development."

For your initial post, address the following questions. What went wrong in the prototyping approach, and at what point could it have been corrected? How does prototype fidelity level influence the type of feedback stakeholders provide? What would you recommend as the correct sequencing of design artifacts for this project? How would you reset stakeholder expectations at this stage without damaging the relationship?
