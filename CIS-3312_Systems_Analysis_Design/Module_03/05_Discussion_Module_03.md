## Scenario A: The Unreliable Document

A BA is assigned to a project replacing a legacy procurement system at a manufacturing company. The BA begins with document analysis, reviewing the existing system's user manual (last updated 2015), a process flowchart (last updated 2018), and a vendor contract that describes how purchase orders must be formatted. After completing the document review, the BA discovers in a follow-up interview that purchasing staff have developed 14 unofficial workarounds since the last manual update and that two of the contract's formatting rules are no longer enforced. The BA now has a stack of documents that partially describe the actual current state.

Sub-questions:

1. Explain how the BA should have anticipated this limitation of document analysis before beginning, and describe one specific preparation step that would have reduced the gap between documented and actual processes.
2. Given what the BA now knows, which elicitation technique should be used next to capture the 14 undocumented workarounds accurately, and why is this the best choice over alternatives?
3. The vendor contract is a legal document that may contain implicit requirements. How should the BA handle the two formatting rules that are no longer enforced — document them as requirements, omit them, or something else? Justify your answer.

## Scenario B: Workshop Dynamics Gone Wrong

A BA facilitates a two-hour requirements workshop for a new customer billing system. The workshop includes 10 stakeholders: the CFO, three billing analysts, two IT architects, the customer service manager, and three senior customer service representatives. The CFO dominates the conversation for the first 45 minutes, proposing specific technical solutions. The billing analysts agree with everything the CFO says even when their facial expressions suggest disagreement. The three customer service representatives have not said a single word. At the 90-minute mark, the BA realizes the session has drifted from requirements elicitation into solution design, and the quietest voices in the room have not been heard.

Sub-questions:

1. Identify at least two specific facilitation techniques the BA could use in the remaining 30 minutes to surface the perspectives of the silent customer service representatives without embarrassing the CFO or creating open conflict.
2. The CFO has been proposing solutions rather than describing business needs. How should the BA redirect this without alienating a high-power stakeholder? Reference the Power-Interest Grid from Module 02 in your response.
3. Evaluate whether the workshop should continue or be adjourned and rescheduled with a different structure. Justify your recommendation.

## Scenario C: The Prototype Trap

A BA is working on a new insurance claims portal for internal claims adjusters. After two rounds of stakeholder interviews produced vague, contradictory requirements, the BA decided to create a low-fidelity paper prototype of the main claims entry screen to give stakeholders something concrete to react to. The approach worked well — stakeholders immediately identified 22 specific requirements they had been unable to articulate verbally. However, three weeks later, when the BA attempts to modify the prototype based on new feedback, the senior claims adjuster says: "Wait — I thought we already decided on this layout. Why are you changing it?"

Sub-questions:

1. Identify the elicitation risk that has materialized in this scenario and explain what the BA should have done at the beginning of the prototyping sessions to prevent it.
2. The senior claims adjuster's comment suggests a misunderstanding about the purpose of the prototype. Describe how the BA should respond to this comment professionally and what communication needs to happen with the broader stakeholder group.
3. Despite this complication, the prototype successfully produced 22 requirements that interviews could not. What does this tell us about the relative strengths and limitations of interviews versus prototyping for this type of stakeholder population?

## A Note from Professor Nash

Every technique in this module is a tool, and like any tool, misapplication is more dangerous than non-use. A workshop where the wrong people dominate produces worse requirements than no workshop at all. A prototype that stakeholders treat as a final design commitment derails the entire project. The skill of elicitation is not just knowing which technique to use — it is knowing how to use it in a room full of people with different agendas, communication styles, and organizational pressures. That is what makes business analysis a genuinely difficult professional discipline.
