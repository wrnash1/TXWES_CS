## Course: CIS-3312 Systems Analysis & Design (IIBA ECBA)

### Introduction
Welcome to **Module 03 – Requirements Elicitation Techniques**! Elicitation is the practice of drawing out information from stakeholders and other sources to understand their needs, constraints, and the context of the business problem. This module aligns directly with BABOK® Guide v3 Knowledge Area 4: Elicitation and Collaboration.

Elicitation is often described as the most human-centered skill in business analysis. The techniques you choose depend on the type of information you need, the number and availability of stakeholders, and the project approach (predictive vs. adaptive). Mastering a variety of elicitation techniques — and knowing when to use each — is essential for the ECBA exam and for real-world BA practice.

---

### 1. High-Yield Glossary
Review these essential definitions carefully. The certification exam expects you to know these concepts inside and out:

*   **Elicitation**: Elicitation is the deliberate process a business analyst uses to identify and collect information about stakeholder needs, current processes, constraints, and desired outcomes. Unlike simply receiving information, elicitation is an active, structured activity that uses techniques such as interviews, workshops, and observation to uncover both stated and unstated needs. Unstated or implied needs are particularly important because stakeholders often assume the BA already knows them.

*   **Interview**: An interview is a one-on-one (or small group) elicitation technique in which the BA asks a series of prepared questions to understand a stakeholder's perspective, role, current workflow, pain points, and needs. Interviews may be structured (fixed question list), semi-structured (guided but flexible), or unstructured (open-ended exploration). They are most effective for gathering detailed, individualized information but are time-intensive when many stakeholders must be interviewed.

*   **Requirements Workshop (Joint Application Design/JAD)**: A requirements workshop is a facilitated, time-boxed meeting that brings multiple stakeholders together to elicit, analyze, and agree on requirements collaboratively. Also known as a Joint Application Design (JAD) session in traditional SDLC contexts, workshops are highly efficient for resolving conflicting requirements, building stakeholder consensus, and accelerating the elicitation process relative to individual interviews.

*   **Observation (Job Shadowing)**: Observation is an elicitation technique in which the BA watches a stakeholder perform their actual job tasks to understand current workflows, pain points, and undocumented workarounds. It is particularly valuable when stakeholders struggle to articulate what they do (tacit knowledge) or when documented procedures do not reflect reality. Active observation includes asking questions while watching; passive observation records without interrupting.

*   **Document Analysis**: Document analysis is the review of existing materials — such as process manuals, system documentation, forms, reports, contracts, and regulations — to extract requirements that are already implicitly defined in organizational artifacts. It is a non-intrusive technique that is especially useful when stakeholders are unavailable or when the analyst needs to understand legacy systems before eliciting desired future-state requirements.

*   **Prototyping**: Prototyping is an elicitation technique that creates a preliminary, low- or high-fidelity model of the proposed system (such as a wireframe, mockup, or working demo) to give stakeholders something concrete to react to. Because stakeholders often find it easier to critique a prototype than to describe requirements from scratch, prototyping is highly effective at surfacing unstated requirements and resolving ambiguities. Iterative prototyping cycles are common in adaptive (Agile) contexts.

### 2. Certification Exam Tips
*   **Technique Selection Scenarios**: The ECBA exam frequently presents a scenario and asks which elicitation technique is most appropriate. Learn these key associations: (1) Tacit or undocumented knowledge → Observation; (2) Need for stakeholder consensus on conflicting requirements → Workshop; (3) Detailed individual perspectives → Interview; (4) Understanding existing system → Document Analysis; (5) Stakeholders cannot describe needs abstractly → Prototyping.
*   **Elicitation vs. Analysis**: The ECBA exam distinguishes between elicitation (gathering raw information) and analysis (interpreting and structuring that information into requirements). Make sure you know which BABOK knowledge area owns each: Elicitation and Collaboration = KA 4; Requirements Analysis and Design Definition = KA 5.
*   **Confirmed vs. Unconfirmed Elicitation Results**: BABOK® distinguishes between elicitation results that are raw and unverified ("unconfirmed") and those that have been reviewed with stakeholders and agreed upon ("confirmed"). The confirmation step is a separate BABOK task — "Confirm Elicitation Results" — and is testable on the exam.
*   **Study Resource**: The IIBA's free Webinar Library at [https://www.iiba.org/professional-development/webinars/](https://www.iiba.org/professional-development/webinars/) includes sessions specifically on elicitation techniques — search for "elicitation" to find recorded sessions aligned to ECBA preparation.
