## Scenario A — The Missing Traceability Matrix

A regional bank is three weeks from the planned go-live date for a new mortgage origination system. The project manager asks the BA for the Requirements Traceability Matrix to assess release readiness. The BA discovers that no RTM was ever created. Requirements were written, the system was built, and testing was conducted — but nobody ever formally linked requirements to test cases or tracked which requirements had been verified.

The project manager's response: "We ran all the test cases and most of them passed. We know the system works. Why do we need the RTM now? It's too late anyway."

The QA lead privately tells the BA that she believes several requirements were never tested at all because the test suite was built informally and she cannot confirm coverage.

For your initial post, address the following questions. What specific risks does the absence of an RTM create at this stage in the project? How would you respond to the project manager's argument? Is it worth attempting to reconstruct the RTM with three weeks remaining, and what would that reconstruction involve? What would you recommend the team do before proceeding to go-live?

## Scenario B — UAT Participation Conflict

A state government agency is implementing a new benefits enrollment system that will be used by thousands of social workers and case managers statewide. The project director has proposed the following UAT plan: the agency's IT department will conduct UAT over two weeks, using IT staff who have been involved in the project since the beginning. The director argues this will be faster and less disruptive than involving frontline social workers.

The BA objects, explaining that having IT staff conduct UAT defeats the purpose of the activity. The project director pushes back: "Our IT people know this system better than anyone. They'll catch more problems."

The BA knows from research and prior experience that IT-conducted UAT consistently misses usability problems, workflow mismatches, and business rule gaps that only real end users would encounter.

For your initial post, address the following questions. What specific types of problems is IT-conducted UAT likely to miss, and why? How would you make the case to the project director that frontline social workers must be included? What is the minimum viable UAT participant mix you would recommend given the time and disruption constraints? What risk statement would you formally document if the project director overrules you?

## Scenario C — The Deferred Defect Decision

A manufacturing company is two days away from the scheduled go-live of a new inventory management system. UAT has gone reasonably well, but three defects remain open:

Defect A: The system incorrectly calculates reorder quantities for items with fractional unit-of-measure conversions. This affects approximately 12% of the inventory catalog. Severity: High.

Defect B: The PDF export of inventory reports shows a misaligned column header on page two of multi-page reports. Severity: Low.

Defect C: When a warehouse manager reassigns an item to a different storage location, the old location record is not cleared, leaving ghost records in the location table. The data team says this can be cleaned up manually with a script run weekly. Severity: Medium.

The VP of Operations wants to go live as scheduled. The development team says fixing Defect A will take three to four days. The project manager asks the BA to recommend a go/no-go decision.

For your initial post, address the following questions. How should each defect influence the go/no-go recommendation? What questions would you ask the business to assess the true impact of Defect A? What conditions, if any, would make deferring Defect A acceptable? How do you communicate a go/no-go recommendation to a VP who has already decided she wants to go live?
