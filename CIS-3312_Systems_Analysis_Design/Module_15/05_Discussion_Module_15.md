## Scenario A — The Skipped Training Plan

A mid-sized insurance company is implementing a new claims processing system. The project manager, facing budget pressure, proposes eliminating the formal training program and replacing it with a two-page quick reference card distributed on go-live day. His reasoning: "Our adjusters are smart. They'll figure it out. We did this with the last system and it was fine."

The BA objects but is overruled. On go-live day, the claims adjusters are overwhelmed. Error rates spike, processing times triple, and by the end of week one the VP of Claims is threatening to roll back the system. The IT director blames the system. The project manager blames the adjusters. The adjusters blame the lack of training.

For your initial post, address the following questions. Using the ADKAR model, identify which stage(s) were inadequately addressed by the quick reference card approach. What specific outcomes were predictable from this training plan failure? What is the BA's professional responsibility when overruled on a decision that creates known, foreseeable risk? What would you recommend at this point — one week post-go-live — to stabilize the situation?

## Scenario B — The Resistance Surprise

A county government is deploying a new permitting system that will replace a twenty-year-old application used by building inspectors, permit clerks, and contractors. The IT department conducted a detailed technical rollout, system testing passed, and training was completed on schedule.

Two months after go-live, adoption metrics show that only 40% of building inspectors are consistently using the new system. The other 60% are submitting handwritten inspection reports that clerks then enter into the system manually — recreating the old workflow. When interviewed, the inspectors say: "The new system is fine but I don't see why we couldn't just keep doing it the way we always have. Nobody ever explained why this needed to change."

The project team is frustrated. They did everything right technically. Nobody anticipated this level of resistance.

For your initial post, address the following questions. Which ADKAR stage was missed for the building inspectors, and at what point in the project should it have been addressed? What does this scenario reveal about the assumptions the project team made during planning? What specific interventions would you recommend now to move the inspectors through the blocked ADKAR stage? What does this scenario suggest about the relationship between technical success and project success?

## Scenario C — The Premature Go-Live

A national logistics company is implementing a new freight tracking system that will replace three separate legacy systems. The project has been under development for eighteen months and the executive sponsor has publicly committed to a go-live date at an industry conference.

Three weeks before go-live, the BA's risk assessment shows: training completion is at 62% of the target user population, twenty-three high-priority defects remain open, integration testing with two external carrier APIs failed this week, and the operations team has not received the transition plan or any knowledge transfer from the project team.

The executive sponsor refuses to delay go-live. "We announced this publicly. Delaying is not an option."

For your initial post, address the following questions. What are the specific, foreseeable risks of proceeding with go-live under these conditions? What is the BA's formal responsibility when go-live readiness criteria have not been met? How would you frame the risk communication to the executive sponsor without directly saying "you are making a mistake"? If go-live proceeds anyway, what emergency measures should be in place to protect operations?
